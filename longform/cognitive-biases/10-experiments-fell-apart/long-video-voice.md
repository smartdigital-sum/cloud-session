## English

**Style instructions**
```
You are an intriguing, slightly mysterious, authoritative narrator for the 4-minute finale of a psychology series, a countdown called "Five famous psychology experiments that fell apart". Speak directly to one listener ("you"), like a sharp, fair guide who respects scientists. Indian English accent. Unhurried, about 140 words per minute, with deliberate pauses between short lines. Open intriguing and knowing. Announce each number clearly with a small lift. Be fair and balanced on each finding, quietly mysterious on the slow-walkers reveal, most serious on the 100-study check-up, then warm, hopeful and authoritative on how science improved. End friendly and grateful. Never mocking, never sensational. Open firm and close to the mic on the first line (never whispered). Lean in on each transition (rehook) line.
```

**Text**
```
[firmly] Some of the most famous "facts" in psychology fell apart when scientists checked them again. And you've probably repeated at least one.
[knowingly] Power poses. The marshmallow test. Willpower as a battery.
[short pause] You've heard them in talks and self-help books. Some of them didn't survive.

[calm] It's about a bias scientists have too: believing exciting results too quickly.
In the last decade or so, they began re-running famous experiments with bigger samples. Let's count down five that didn't hold up the way we were told.
[short pause] [intrigued] Number one is the biggest of all. And at the end, what still stands.

[curious] Number five: the marshmallow test.
In famous studies from Stanford, children who waited longer for a second treat seemed to do better years later. The message spread: self-control at four predicts success.
But in 2018, researchers re-tested the idea with around nine hundred children, and accounted for things like family background. The link shrank by about two-thirds.
[short pause] Waiting still mattered a little. Background mattered a lot.

[knowingly] Number four: power posing.
A 2010 study claimed that standing like a superhero for two minutes could change your hormones and make you take more risks. It became one of the most-watched talks ever.
But a larger study in 2015 found no hormone changes. And in 2016, one of the original authors, Dana Carney, publicly wrote that she no longer believed the effects were real.
[short pause] Standing tall may help you feel a bit more confident. The hormone story didn't hold.

[playfully] The next one sounds almost like magic. [short pause] Which should have been the first clue.

[mysterious] Number three: the slow walkers.
In a famous 1996 study, people who unscrambled words linked to old age, like "wrinkle" and "Florida", supposedly walked more slowly down the hallway afterwards.
In 2012, a team in Belgium repeated it, timing the walk with infrared sensors. No effect. The slow walking only appeared when the experimenters expected it.
[short pause] The experimenters' own beliefs may have shaped the result.

[thoughtful] Number two: willpower as a battery.
The idea: self-control uses up a limited fuel, so resisting cookies leaves you weaker for the next task. Hundreds of studies seemed to agree.
Then in 2016, twenty-three labs tested it together, with about two thousand people. The effect was close to zero. Some researchers argue the method was the problem, and the debate isn't over.
[short pause] But the simple "battery" story is far shakier than the books said.

[serious] And number one isn't a single study. [short pause] It's a hundred of them.

[serious] And number one: the discovery behind all of this.
In 2015, two hundred and seventy researchers tried to repeat one hundred published psychology studies. Only about thirty-six percent produced significant results again. And on average, the effects were about half as big.
[short pause] It wasn't one bad study. It was a wake-up call for a whole field.

[warmly] But here's the hopeful twist: science noticed, and fixed its habits. Bigger samples. Plans registered before the data comes in. Many labs testing the same idea.
And some findings passed: anchoring came back strong across dozens of labs, and the better-than-average effect repeated with over a thousand drivers.
[long pause] So when you hear a surprising study, ask: how big was it, and has anyone repeated it?

[friendly] That's why, in this series, we always told you when a study was debated.
Tell me in the comments: which of these five did you believe?
[short pause] Thanks for watching the whole series.
```
