## HOOK (0:00–0:10)

**[NARRATION]**
Some of the most famous "facts" in psychology fell apart when scientists checked them again. And you've probably repeated at least one.
Power poses. The marshmallow test. Willpower as a battery.
(pause) You've heard them in talks and self-help books. Some of them didn't survive.

**[VISUAL]** Frame 1 is already moving: three glossy self-help book covers float in: a superhero pose silhouette, a marshmallow on a plate, a battery icon. A crack runs down each cover; small pieces fall away.

_Tone: firm on line one; intriguing and knowing on the list._

## LOCK-IN (0:10–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
It's about a bias scientists have too: believing exciting results too quickly.
In the last decade or so, they began re-running famous experiments with bigger samples. Let's count down five that didn't hold up the way we were told.
(pause) Number one is the biggest of all. And at the end, what still stands.

**[VISUAL]** A lab bench with old journals; a new team re-runs an experiment with a much larger crowd. Stamps alternate on old papers: HELD UP ✓ and DIDN'T HOLD ✗. Countdown card: **5 · 4 · 3 · 2 · 1**, the 1 covered with a cloth.

_Tone: knowing; clear promise; intrigued on "the biggest of all"._

## MAIN BODY (COUNTDOWN)

### BODY 1: #5 The marshmallow test (0:40–1:05)

**[NARRATION]**
Number five: the marshmallow test.
In famous studies from Stanford, children who waited longer for a second treat seemed to do better years later. The message spread: self-control at four predicts success.
But in 2018, researchers re-tested the idea with around nine hundred children, and accounted for things like family background. The link shrank by about two-thirds.
(pause) Waiting still mattered a little. Background mattered a lot.

**[VISUAL]** A child at a table staring at one marshmallow, a timer ticking. A thick arrow WAITING → SUCCESS. Then extra arrows appear: FAMILY BACKGROUND, HOME, EARLY ABILITY; the original arrow thins to a third of its width. Caption: _Watts, Duncan & Quan, 2018_.

_Tone: curious, fair; balanced last line._

### BODY 2: #4 Power posing (1:05–1:35)

**[NARRATION]**
Number four: power posing.
A 2010 study claimed that standing like a superhero for two minutes could change your hormones and make you take more risks. It became one of the most-watched talks ever.
But a larger study in 2015 found no hormone changes. And in 2016, one of the original authors, Dana Carney, publicly wrote that she no longer believed the effects were real.
(pause) Standing tall may help you feel a bit more confident. The hormone story didn't hold.

**[VISUAL]** A figure in a hands-on-hips pose; a stopwatch at 2:00; hormone bottles rising. Then a bigger lab: the bottles stay flat. A typed website statement fades in (stylised, not a real screenshot): "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real." Caption: _Carney, 2016_. The figure relaxes into a normal, comfortable stance.

_Tone: knowing, respectful; careful on the last line._

### REHOOK 1 (1:35)

**[NARRATION]**
The next one sounds almost like magic. (pause) Which should have been the first clue.

**[VISUAL]** A wand sparkles over an old journal page, then fizzles.

_Tone: playful, knowing._

### BODY 3: #3 The slow walkers (1:35–2:05)

**[NARRATION]**
Number three: the slow walkers.
In a famous 1996 study, people who unscrambled words linked to old age, like "wrinkle" and "Florida", supposedly walked more slowly down the hallway afterwards.
In 2012, a team in Belgium repeated it, timing the walk with infrared sensors. No effect. The slow walking only appeared when the experimenters expected it.
(pause) The experimenters' own beliefs may have shaped the result.

**[VISUAL]** Word cards (WRINKLE, FLORIDA, BINGO) shuffle on a table. A long hallway; a person walks to the end. Version 1: a stopwatch held by an experimenter. Version 2: infrared beams across the hallway record the time; the bars are equal. A thought bubble over an experimenter: "they'll be slower…" and only then does the bar lengthen. Captions: _Bargh et al., 1996_ · _Doyen et al., 2012_.

_Tone: mysterious, then a quiet reveal._

### BODY 4: #2 Willpower as a battery (2:05–2:35)

**[NARRATION]**
Number two: willpower as a battery.
The idea: self-control uses up a limited fuel, so resisting cookies leaves you weaker for the next task. Hundreds of studies seemed to agree.
Then in 2016, twenty-three labs tested it together, with about two thousand people. The effect was close to zero. Some researchers argue the method was the problem, and the debate isn't over.
(pause) But the simple "battery" story is far shakier than the books said.

**[VISUAL]** A battery labelled WILLPOWER drains as a figure resists a plate of cookies. Then a world map with 23 lab pins; a combined result dial points at ~0. Two scholar silhouettes debate at a whiteboard: METHOD? vs EFFECT? Caption: _Hagger et al., 2016 (registered replication)_.

_Tone: thoughtful, fair; firm last line._

### REHOOK 2 (2:35)

**[NARRATION]**
And number one isn't a single study. (pause) It's a hundred of them.

**[VISUAL]** The cloth whips off the 1: a stack of 100 papers.

_Tone: serious, leaning in._

### BODY 5: #1 The 100-study check-up (2:35–3:05)

**[NARRATION]**
And number one: the discovery behind all of this.
In 2015, two hundred and seventy researchers tried to repeat one hundred published psychology studies. Only about thirty-six percent produced significant results again. And on average, the effects were about half as big.
(pause) It wasn't one bad study. It was a wake-up call for a whole field.

**[VISUAL]** 100 small journal icons in a grid; 36 light up green. A bar showing ORIGINAL EFFECT and a second bar about half as tall labelled REPLICATION. An alarm clock rings over a university building. Caption: _Open Science Collaboration, 2015, Science_.

_Tone: serious, the most weight in the video._

## CLOSING (3:05–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
But here's the hopeful twist: science noticed, and fixed its habits. Bigger samples. Plans registered before the data comes in. Many labs testing the same idea.
And some findings passed: anchoring came back strong across dozens of labs, and the better-than-average effect repeated with over a thousand drivers.
(pause) So when you hear a surprising study, ask: how big was it, and has anyone repeated it?

**[VISUAL]** A toolbox opens: BIG SAMPLES · PRE-REGISTRATION · MANY LABS. Two badges glow: ANCHORING ✓ (wheel from video 03) and BETTER-THAN-AVERAGE ✓ (traffic bubbles from video 08). Final card with two questions: HOW BIG? · REPEATED?

_Tone: warm, hopeful, authoritative (peak); clear final question._
