## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The 1999 study (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1999, psychologists Justin Kruger and David Dunning at Cornell University tested people on logic, grammar and humour. Then they asked: how well do you think you did, compared to everyone else?
People who scored in the bottom quarter on the logic test were, on average, around the 12th percentile. But they guessed they were around the 62nd.
(pause) Their score was near the bottom. Their confidence was above average.

**[VISUAL]** Three test papers: LOGIC · GRAMMAR · HUMOUR. A percentile ladder from 0 to 100: a small figure stands at rung 12, with a thought bubble pointing up to rung 62. Caption: _Kruger & Dunning, 1999, Cornell_.

_Tone: clear, documentary; a beat of quiet irony on the last line._

### BODY 2: The double burden (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Dunning and Kruger's explanation was elegant. The skills you need to do something well are often the same skills you need to notice you're doing it badly.
If you don't know grammar, you can't spot your own grammar mistakes. It's like singing off-key without hearing it, because hearing the right note is the very skill that's missing. They called it a double burden: you're wrong, and you can't see that you're wrong.
(pause) But that's not the whole picture.

**[VISUAL]** A sentence with three grammar errors; a figure looks at it and sees a big green tick. A second figure with a "grammar lens" sees the red marks. Title card: **THE DOUBLE BURDEN**. A curtain twitches at the edge of the frame.

_Tone: thoughtful; a mysterious turn on the last line._

### REHOOK 1 (1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
But that's only half the graph. (pause) The other half is the part nobody shares.

**[VISUAL]** The graph's right side, faded until now, lights up.

### BODY 3: "Mount Stupid" and the forgotten finding (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
First, that famous graph. A hump of confidence, a valley, a slow climb. It's an internet drawing. Nothing like it appears in the 1999 paper.
Second, the study found something people forget: the top performers underestimated themselves. They assumed tasks that were easy for them were easy for everyone.
(pause) So the effect isn't just "fools are confident". Almost everyone guessed they were somewhere near the middle.

**[VISUAL]** The meme curve peels off the chalkboard like a sticker. Underneath: a simple chart of four groups. The bottom group's guess sits far above their score; the top group's guess sits a little *below* their score; all four guesses bunch near the middle. Label: **EVERYONE DRIFTS TO THE MIDDLE**.

_Tone: intrigued, revealing._

### REHOOK 2 (2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
And then statisticians asked a harder question. (pause) Is the effect even real?

**[VISUAL]** A scatter of random dots slides over the graph.

_Tone: serious, leaning in._

### BODY 4: The statisticians step in (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
And then statisticians stepped in. Some showed that when you plot even random numbers this way, you can get a similar pattern, because of noise and "regression to the mean".
A 2020 study of over 900 people found little sign of the classic effect when it used stricter methods. Other researchers, using large datasets, still find a smaller but real effect.
(pause) So the honest answer: the pattern exists, but it's smaller, and less dramatic, than the meme.

**[VISUAL]** Dice roll random numbers into a scatter plot; the same four-group chart emerges from pure noise, with an eerie match. Caption: _random data_. Then two scholar silhouettes on either side of a scale: "MOSTLY ARTEFACT" vs "SMALL BUT REAL"; the scale settles slightly toward real but much smaller than the meme's mountain. Captions: _Gignac & Zajenkowski, 2020_ · _Dunkel et al., 2023_.

_Tone: serious, fair; balanced on the last line._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
Here's the lesson that survives all the debate. Everyone, not just "other people", is bad at judging their own skill.
The fix isn't to call others stupid. It's to check yourself: ask for feedback, test your knowledge, and notice when you're most sure. Before your next argument, ask yourself one question: "What would change my mind?"
(pause) Certainty is a feeling. It isn't evidence.

**[VISUAL]** A mirror appears where the meme was; the viewer-figure looks into it. Three cards: **ASK FOR FEEDBACK · TEST YOURSELF · NOTICE CERTAINTY**. Final card in large letters: CERTAINTY ≠ EVIDENCE.

_Tone: warm, authoritative (peak); slow, weighty last line._

## Fact check

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Kruger & Dunning (1999), Cornell; tests of logic, grammar and humour | ✓ *J. Personality & Social Psychology* 77(6), "Unskilled and unaware of it" | [Wikipedia summary](https://ipfs.ssi.eecc.de/ipns/en.wikipedia-on-ipfs.org/wiki/Dunning%E2%80%93Kruger_effect) |
| Bottom quartile on logic: ~12th percentile actual vs ~62nd estimated | ✓ widely documented from the paper | [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/why-we-think-we-know-more-than-we-do-1283150) |
| Skills to perform = skills to judge performance ("double burden") | ✓ the authors' explanation | as above |
| Top performers underestimated themselves | ✓ reported in the original paper | as above |
| "Mount Stupid" graph not in the 1999 paper | ✓ the paper uses quartile plots of perceived vs actual percentile; the hump-and-valley curve is a later internet illustration | [IDR Labs: "There is no Mount Stupid"](https://www.idrlabs.com/articles/?p=7750) |
| Random data can produce a similar pattern (noise, regression to the mean) | ✓ critiques incl. Nuhfer et al.; Magnus & Peresetsky (2022) | [CASRAI critique summary](https://casrai.org/news/the-dunning-kruger-effect-study-metric-critique) |
| 2020 study (929 people) found little evidence with stricter methods | ✓ Gignac & Zajenkowski (2020), *Intelligence* | [X-MOL record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1249535459370885120) |
| Others still find a smaller but real effect with large datasets | ✓ e.g. Dunkel, Nedelec & van der Linden (2023) | [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/2023-dunkel.pdf) |
