**Text**
```
[playfully] Quick question. Compared to other drivers on the road, are you better than average?
[short pause] If you said yes, you're in very good company. Maybe too good.

[curious] Have you ever noticed how everyone else drives badly, but you're careful? How the rest of the team is slow, but you carry the work?
Psychologists call this the better-than-average effect. At work, in exams, even in how good a friend we think we are.
[short pause] And it shows up almost everywhere they look.

[intrigued] In 1981, Swedish psychologist Ola Svenson asked students in the US and Sweden to compare their driving skill to everyone else in the room.
Ninety-three percent of the Americans said they were more skilful than the median driver. In Sweden, sixty-nine percent.
[short pause] But only half of any group can be above the median. That's what the median means. Think about that room: if almost everyone is above the middle, who's below it?

[thoughtful] This isn't just an old quirk. In 2023, researchers repeated the study with over twelve hundred people, and found the same pattern: most rated themselves well above the middle.
And it's not just driving. People tend to rate themselves above average at being kind, honest, and good with others.
[playfully] There's even a nickname: the Lake Wobegon effect, after a fictional town where "all the children are above average".

[thoughtful] Why does this happen? One reason: you see your own intentions, but only other people's actions.
You know you cut in because you were late for something important. They just look rude.
Another: we each define "good" in our own way. A careful driver counts safety. A fast driver counts skill. Both win their own contest. Memory helps too: you remember your skilful moments, and forget the near-misses.
[short pause] And feeling capable just feels good.

[intrigued] Now, two honest twists. First, the maths isn't always impossible: if a few people are terrible, most people can be above the average, the mean. Svenson asked about the median, which is why his result is so striking.
Second, the deepest version: most people also believe they're less biased than average.
[short pause] We're experts at spotting bias… in everyone else.

[warmly] So should you stop believing in yourself? No. A little confidence helps you try hard things.
But for decisions that matter, like driving, money or health, swap the feeling for a measure. Ask for honest feedback. Compare with real data, not your memory. Even experts do this: it's one reason pilots and surgeons use checklists, not just confidence.
And when you think "I'm better than most", ask: "How would I know?"
[long pause] Confidence is fuel. Just don't use it as a map.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what's one thing you were sure you were above average at, until you checked?
And subscribe. Next: why one insult can outweigh ten compliments.
```
