## HOOK (0:00–0:10)

**[NARRATION]**
Almost everyone believes they're above average. And that belief quietly leads to bad decisions.
Quick question. Compared to other drivers on the road, are you better than average?
(pause) If you said yes, you're in very good company. Maybe too good.

**[VISUAL]** Frame 1 is already moving: a busy Indian city road at dusk. A thought bubble pops above every single vehicle: "I'm a good driver." The camera rises; hundreds of identical bubbles float over the traffic.

_Tone: firm on line one; playful and knowing on the question._

## LOCK-IN (0:10–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever noticed how everyone else drives badly, but you're careful? Psychologists call this the better-than-average effect.
In this video: a 1981 driving study, a 2023 repeat, and why your brain does it. Then two honest twists.
(pause) And a simple question for the decisions that matter.

**[VISUAL]** A figure glares at a car cutting in, then calmly does the same move with a halo over their head. Title card: **BETTER-THAN-AVERAGE EFFECT**. Three sealed cards: **1981 · 2023 · WHY**. Two small cards **TWIST 1 · TWIST 2** and a glowing **THE QUESTION** stay sealed.

_Tone: knowing; clear promise; firm on the last line._

## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The 1981 driving study (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1981, Swedish psychologist Ola Svenson asked students in the US and Sweden to compare their driving skill to everyone else in the room.
Ninety-three percent of the Americans said they were more skilful than the median driver. In Sweden, sixty-nine percent.
(pause) But only half of any group can be above the median. That's what the median means. Think about that room: if almost everyone is above the middle, who's below it?

**[VISUAL]** Two classrooms (US flag, Swedish flag). Hands go up: 93 of 100 figures in the US room, 69 of 100 in the Swedish room. A line of 100 figures sorted by skill; a glowing marker in the exact middle labelled MEDIAN; only 50 can stand to its right. Caption: _Svenson, 1981_.

_Tone: intrigued; clear, slightly amused on the maths line._

### BODY 2: Still true in 2023, and not just driving (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
This isn't just an old quirk. In 2023, researchers repeated the study with over twelve hundred people, and found the same pattern: most rated themselves well above the middle.
And it's not just driving. People tend to rate themselves above average at being kind, honest, and good with others.
There's even a nickname: the Lake Wobegon effect, after a fictional town where "all the children are above average".

**[VISUAL]** A 2023 dataset grid: 1,203 dots, most clustered on the "above median" side. Trait cards flip: KIND · HONEST · GOOD WITH PEOPLE, each with a crowd of raised hands. A storybook town sign: LAKE WOBEGON, "ALL THE CHILDREN ARE ABOVE AVERAGE". Caption: _Replication of Svenson (2023), Metapsychology_.

_Tone: thoughtful; playful on the nickname._

### REHOOK 1 (1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
So why does almost everyone feel above average? (pause) It isn't just ego.

**[VISUAL]** The halo over the figure flickers; a window into their head shows good intentions.

_Tone: curious, leaning in._

### BODY 3: Why we all feel above average (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Why does this happen? One reason: you see your own intentions, but only other people's actions.
You know you cut in because you were late for something important. They just look rude.
Another: we each define "good" in our own way. A careful driver counts safety. A fast driver counts skill. Both win their own contest. Memory helps too: you remember your skilful moments, and forget the near-misses.
(pause) And feeling capable just feels good.

**[VISUAL]** Split view: inside the figure's car, a thought bubble shows a hospital appointment and a clock; outside, another car's driver has no bubble at all, just a frown emoji drawn over them. Then two trophies: SAFEST (careful driver) and MOST SKILFUL (fast driver), each driver holding their own. A warm glow on the figure's face.

_Tone: thoughtful, gently humorous._

### REHOOK 2 (2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Now, two twists most videos skip. (pause) And one of them is about you, watching this.

**[VISUAL]** The camera turns toward the viewer; a small mirror slides in.

_Tone: knowing, a little playful._

### BODY 4: Two honest twists (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Now, two honest twists. First, the maths isn't always impossible: if a few people are terrible, most people can be above the average, the mean. Svenson asked about the median, which is why his result is so striking.
Second, the deepest version: most people also believe they're less biased than average.
(pause) We're experts at spotting bias… in everyone else.

**[VISUAL]** A bar chart where three very low bars drag the mean line down, so most bars sit above it; the MEDIAN marker stays in the middle. Then a figure holds a magnifying glass up to a crowd, spotting "BIAS!" everywhere, while their own reflection in the glass stays blank. Caption: _the "bias blind spot" (Pronin et al., 2002)_.

_Tone: intrigued, fair; a knowing pause before "in everyone else."_

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So should you stop believing in yourself? No. A little confidence helps you try hard things.
But for decisions that matter, like driving, money or health, swap the feeling for a measure. Ask for honest feedback. Compare with real data, not your memory. Even experts do this: it's one reason pilots and surgeons use checklists, not just confidence.
And when you think "I'm better than most", ask: "How would I know?"
(pause) Confidence is fuel. Just don't use it as a map.

**[VISUAL]** A fuel gauge labelled CONFIDENCE glows green as the figure starts a hard climb. Then a map unrolls labelled DATA · FEEDBACK · RESULTS, and the figure checks it at a fork in the road. Final card: CONFIDENCE = FUEL · DATA = MAP.

_Tone: warm, authoritative (peak); crisp last line._

## Fact check

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Svenson (1981): 93% of US and 69% of Swedish students rated themselves more skilful than the median driver in their group | ✓ *Acta Psychologica* 47 (some sources say 68% for Sweden) | [Metapsychology replication](https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/article/view/2932) |
| 2023 replication (N = 1,203) found the same pattern | ✓ "We are all less risky and more skillful than our fellow drivers: successful replication and extension of Svenson (1981)", *Metapsychology* | as above |
| People rate themselves above average on positive traits (kindness, honesty, getting along) | ✓ better-than-average effect (e.g. Alicke, 1985); worded as "tend to" | general literature |
| "Lake Wobegon effect" nickname from Garrison Keillor's fictional town | ✓ | general |
| Own intentions vs others' actions; idiosyncratic definitions of "good" | ✓ standard explanations (e.g. Dunning, Meyerowitz & Holzberg, 1989 on trait ambiguity) | general literature |
| Mean vs median: most can be above the mean if a few are very low; Svenson asked about the median | ✓ statistics | — |
| Most people believe they are less biased than average ("bias blind spot") | ✓ Pronin, Lin & Ross (2002), *PSPB*; ⚠ no percentage quoted | general literature |
