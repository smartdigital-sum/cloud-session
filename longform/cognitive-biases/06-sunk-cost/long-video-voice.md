**Text**
```
[knowingly] Twenty minutes into a terrible movie, you know it's bad.
[short pause] And you stay. Not because it'll get better. Because you already paid.

[curious] Have you ever stayed in a course, a project, even a relationship, mostly because of how much you'd already put in?
That's the sunk cost fallacy: money, time or effort that's already gone, quietly steering what you do next.
[short pause] And it's been caught in a real theatre, in a supersonic jet, and maybe even in mice.

[intrigued] In 1982, psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran an experiment at the Ohio University theatre.
People buying season tickets were randomly given one of three prices: the full price, a small discount, or a bigger discount.
Same plays. Same seats. The only difference was what they'd paid.
Over the next six months, the people who'd paid full price went to more plays than those with discounts. By the second half of the season, the difference faded.
[short pause] Paying more didn't make the plays better. It made walking away feel worse.

[thoughtful] Why? Because quitting turns a cost into a loss. As long as you keep going, the money still feels "used". The moment you stop, it feels "wasted".
And as we saw in the loss aversion video, losses feel louder. There's also pride: stopping can feel like admitting you were wrong.
[playfully] It's the same feeling that makes you finish a buffet plate you don't even want, because you have to get your "paisa vasool".

[serious] Governments do it too. Britain and France kept pouring money into Concorde, the supersonic passenger jet, long after it was clear it would struggle to make money.
So many years and so much money had gone in, stopping felt impossible. Biologists even borrowed the name: the "Concorde fallacy".
[warmly] And closer to home, think of a coaching class you hate but keep attending, because the fees are already paid.

[mysterious] For a long time, researchers thought animals were too sensible for this mistake.
Then, in 2018, a study tested mice, rats and humans on similar waiting tasks. In all three, the longer they had already waited, the more likely they were to keep waiting for the reward.
[short pause] This pull may be older than money itself.

[warmly] So here's how to break it. Ask one question: "If I were starting fresh today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this?"
If the answer is no, the money or time is already gone, whatever you do next. Leaving doesn't waste it. Staying just adds to it.
[long pause] The past is a cost. Don't let it become a plan.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what's something you finished only because you'd already started?
And subscribe. Next: why smart people fall for wrong ideas, and a simple number puzzle most people get wrong the same way.
```
