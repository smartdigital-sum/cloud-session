## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The theatre ticket experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1982, psychologists Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer ran an experiment at the Ohio University theatre.
People buying season tickets were randomly given one of three prices: the full price, a small discount, or a bigger discount.
Same plays. Same seats. The only difference was what they'd paid.
Over the next six months, the people who'd paid full price went to more plays than those with discounts. By the second half of the season, the difference faded.
(pause) Paying more didn't make the plays better. It made walking away feel worse.

**[VISUAL]** A 1980s box office window; three ticket stubs: FULL PRICE, SMALL DISCOUNT, BIG DISCOUNT, handed out by a spinning randomiser. A season calendar: in the first months, the full-price group's attendance bar is clearly higher; later the bars even out. Caption: _Arkes & Blumer, 1985 (Ohio University Theater, 1982–83 season)_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; honest on the fade; pointed last line._

### BODY 2: Why quitting feels like losing (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Why? Because quitting turns a cost into a loss. As long as you keep going, the money still feels "used". The moment you stop, it feels "wasted".
And as we saw in the loss aversion video, losses feel louder. There's also pride: stopping can feel like admitting you were wrong.
It's the same feeling that makes you finish a buffet plate you don't even want, because you have to get your "paisa vasool".

**[VISUAL]** A cost receipt; as the figure stands to leave, the receipt turns red and stamps itself LOSS. The loud LOSSES megaphone from video 04 makes a cameo. A small crown labelled PRIDE wobbles on the figure's head. Final shot: an overloaded buffet plate being finished with a grimace.

_Tone: thoughtful; playful on "paisa vasool"._

### BODY 3: The Concorde fallacy (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Governments do it too. Britain and France kept pouring money into Concorde, the supersonic passenger jet, long after it was clear it would struggle to make money.
So many years and so much money had gone in, stopping felt impossible. Biologists even borrowed the name: the "Concorde fallacy".
And closer to home, think of a coaching class you hate but keep attending, because the fees are already paid.

**[VISUAL]** A sleek white supersonic jet on a runway; banknotes stream into its engines. A long timeline of years scrolls behind it. Label pops up: **"CONCORDE FALLACY"**. Cut to a student dragging their feet into a coaching centre while a "FEES PAID ✓" receipt flutters behind them.

_Tone: serious, slightly wry; relatable on the coaching line._

### BODY 4: Older than money? (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
For a long time, researchers thought animals were too sensible for this mistake.
Then, in 2018, a study tested mice, rats and humans on similar waiting tasks. In all three, the longer they had already waited, the more likely they were to keep waiting for the reward.
(pause) This pull may be older than money itself.

**[VISUAL]** Three side-by-side "waiting rooms": a mouse in a maze corridor, a rat at a food port, a human watching a video-loading bar. Each has a timer counting up; as the timer grows, each one leans in rather than leaving. Caption: _Sweis et al., 2018_. A faint, ancient cave-painting texture washes over the frame.

_Tone: mysterious, intrigued; quiet last line._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So here's how to break it. Ask one question: "If I were starting fresh today, knowing what I know now, would I choose this?"
If the answer is no, the money or time is already gone, whatever you do next. Leaving doesn't waste it. Staying just adds to it.
(pause) The past is a cost. Don't let it become a plan.

**[VISUAL]** A clean, blank page labelled TODAY. The heavy ALREADY SPENT backpack unclips and drops away. The figure walks out of the cinema into daylight, then picks a new path on a signpost.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); firm, gentle last line._
