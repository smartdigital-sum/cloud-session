## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The left-digit effect (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
We read numbers left to right. And the brain gives the first digit far more weight than the rest.
By the time you reach the nines, your mind has already filed the price under "four hundred".
Researchers call this the left-digit effect. In lab studies, a price feels noticeably smaller when its first digit drops, like 2.99 versus 3.00, but not when only the later digits change.
Quick test: which feels bigger, the gap between ₹299 and ₹300, or between ₹301 and ₹302? Same one rupee. But the first one feels like a jump.

**[VISUAL]** An eye scans "₹499" left to right; the "4" lands in a big drawer labelled 400s and the drawer slams shut before the 9s arrive. A split card: 2.99 vs 3.00 (big perceived gap) and 3.59 vs 3.60 (no gap). Caption: _Thomas & Morwitz, 2005_.

_Tone: clear, explanatory._

### Beat 2: The catalogue experiments (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Shops discovered this long before scientists measured it.
In field experiments with a US clothing catalogue, economists Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester found that prices ending in 9 lifted sales. Today, look at almost any sale banner in India, and you'll see the same nines.
In one test, a dress sold more at $39 than at a cheaper $34.
(pause) A higher price, and more buyers.

**[VISUAL]** A glossy mail-order catalogue page with the same dress printed in three versions: $34, $39, $44. Order slips fly in; the $39 pile grows tallest. Caption: _Anderson & Simester, 2003_. A small "?!" pops over the $34 pile.

_Tone: playful surprise on the last line._

### Beat 3: Even professional car dealers (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
And it's not just shoppers in a hurry.
In 2012, economists studied over 22 million used cars sold at wholesale auctions in the US, where many buyers are professional dealers.
Prices dropped sharply every time the odometer crossed a 10,000-mile mark. A car at 79,900 miles sold for noticeably more than one at 80,100. These were people who buy cars for a living, with time to inspect every vehicle. And still, the first digit pulled the price.
(pause) Two hundred miles. A big price gap. Because the first digit changed.

**[VISUAL]** An auction lot of cars; an odometer rolls 79,900 → 80,100. A price line chart shows a staircase: flat, then a sudden drop at each 10,000-mile line (illustrative). Caption: _Lacetera, Pope & Sydnor, 2012_. A dealer silhouette scratches their head.

_Tone: serious, slightly incredulous on the last line._

### Beat 4: When nines backfire (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
But nines aren't magic everywhere. A price ending in 9 can also whisper "discount", which is why many luxury shops prefer clean, round numbers.
And how strong the effect is depends on the product and the shopper. Some studies even suggest round prices feel "right" when we buy with our heart, like a gift, while precise prices feel smarter when we buy with our head.
(pause) Still, when someone wants you to feel a price is smaller, watch the first digit.

**[VISUAL]** Split shopfront: a bargain store with ₹999 signs vs a minimalist luxury boutique with a single tag reading ₹5,000. A small whisper bubble from the ₹999 tag: "discount…". Then the eye from Beat 1 returns and focuses on the first digit.

_Tone: thoughtful, balanced; knowing on the last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's your defence. Round up, in your head, every time. ₹499 is ₹500. ₹19,999 is ₹20,000. ₹99 a month is about ₹1,200 a year.
And compare by unit, not by sticker.
(pause) The shop chose that number for your brain. You can choose to read it properly.

**[VISUAL]** The price tags from the hook flip: ₹499 → ₹500, ₹19,999 → ₹20,000, "₹99/month" → "≈ ₹1,200/year". A shopper calmly compares two packs by "₹ per 100 g". The balance scale from the hook levels out.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); slow last line._
