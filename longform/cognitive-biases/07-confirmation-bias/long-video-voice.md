**Text**
```
[firmly] Being smart won't protect you from this mistake. Here's a puzzle to prove it.
[mysterious] I'm thinking of a rule. Two, four, six fits it.
[short pause] Your job: guess the rule. You can test any three numbers you like. Most people get this wrong, in exactly the same way.

[knowingly] Have you ever noticed how, once you believe something, you see proof of it everywhere?
[playfully] In this video: you'll play a famous puzzle from 1960, see how twenty-three out of twenty-nine people fell for it, and where you do the same thing every day.
[short pause] [firmly] And the one question that gets you out.

[intrigued] This is a real experiment, run by psychologist Peter Wason in 1960.
Most people guess "numbers going up by two". So they test eight, ten, twelve. "Yes, fits." Twenty, twenty-two, twenty-four. "Yes." A hundred, a hundred and two, a hundred and four. "Yes!"
Each "yes" makes them more confident. Feeling sure, they announce: "Even numbers going up by two."
[short pause] Wrong. The rule was simply: any three numbers in increasing order.

[thoughtful] In Wason's study, only six out of twenty-nine people found the right rule on their first try.
The problem wasn't intelligence. It was strategy. People only tested examples they expected to fit. Many kept announcing new, more complicated rules, sure they were getting closer.
Nobody tried one, two, three, or ten, seven, one: numbers that could prove their idea wrong.
[short pause] Every "yes" felt like proof. But it was proof of nothing.

[knowingly] You might think: it's just a number game. [short pause] But you play it every day.

[knowingly] Now look at real life. You believe a friend is upset with you, so every short reply looks like evidence.
You like a phone brand, so you click the good reviews and skip the bad ones. And social feeds learn what you agree with, and show you more of it.
Doctors, detectives and scientists are trained to fight this, because it's so natural.
[short pause] It feels like research. Often, it's just collecting "yes".

[curious] So what does the puzzle really show? [short pause] Scientists still argue about that.

[thoughtful] To be fair, some researchers say the two-four-six task shows something slightly different: a habit of testing positive examples, which is often a sensible shortcut in daily life.
Wason himself called it confirmation bias, and the name stuck.
[short pause] Either way, the trap is the same: if you only ask questions that can say "yes", you'll never hear "no".

[warmly] So here's the habit that protects you. Before you decide you're right, try to prove yourself wrong. Scientists have a name for this: trying to falsify your idea.
Ask: "What would I expect to see if I were mistaken?" Then go and look for that. Read one strong argument from the other side.
[long pause] A belief that survives a real test is worth keeping. One that's never been tested is just a guess.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what was your first guess for two, four, six? Be honest.
[short pause] And next: why almost everyone thinks they're an above-average driver, and what that says about all of us.
```
