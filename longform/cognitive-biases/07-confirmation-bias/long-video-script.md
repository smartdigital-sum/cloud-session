## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever noticed how, once you believe something, you suddenly see proof of it everywhere?
That's confirmation bias: we look for, notice and remember the evidence that agrees with us. It doesn't only trap careless people. Being smart doesn't protect you from it.
(pause) Let's play the game.

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The puzzle, played out (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
This is a real experiment, run by psychologist Peter Wason in 1960.
Most people guess "numbers going up by two". So they test 8, 10, 12. "Yes, fits." 20, 22, 24. "Yes." 100, 102, 104. "Yes!"
Each "yes" makes them more confident. Feeling sure, they announce: "Even numbers going up by two."
(pause) Wrong. The rule was simply: any three numbers in increasing order.

**[VISUAL]** A 1960s lab: a volunteer writes triples on cards; the experimenter stamps ✓ ✓ ✓. A confidence meter climbs with each tick. The volunteer proudly holds up "+2 EVEN NUMBERS". A loud buzzer: ✗. The true rule appears: **ANY ASCENDING NUMBERS**. Caption: _Wason, 1960_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; rising confidence; a beat of silence before "Wrong."_

### Beat 2: Only 6 out of 29 (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
In Wason's study, only 6 out of 29 people found the right rule on their first try.
The problem wasn't intelligence. It was strategy. People only tested examples they expected to fit. Many kept announcing new, more complicated rules, sure they were getting closer.
Nobody tried 1, 2, 3, or 10, 7, 1: numbers that could prove their idea wrong.
(pause) Every "yes" felt like proof. But it was proof of nothing.

**[VISUAL]** 29 small figures; only 6 light up. Then two paths: a well-trodden path labelled TESTS THAT SAY "YES" (8-10-12, 20-22-24) and an empty, overgrown path labelled TESTS THAT COULD SAY "NO" (1-2-3, 10-7-1). A pile of ✓ ticks collapses into dust.

_Tone: thoughtful; quiet emphasis on the last line._

### Beat 3: Collecting "yes" in real life (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Now look at real life. You believe a friend is upset with you, so every short reply looks like evidence.
You like a phone brand, so you click the good reviews and skip the bad ones. And social feeds learn what you agree with, and show you more of it.
Doctors, detectives and scientists are trained to fight this, because it's so natural.
(pause) It feels like research. Often, it's just collecting "yes".

**[VISUAL]** A chat with "ok." in it, magnified under a detective's lens. A review page where the figure's cursor skips the 1-star reviews. A feed that slowly turns one colour. A doctor and a detective (silhouettes) holding a checklist titled WHAT WOULD PROVE ME WRONG? A basket filling with "YES" tokens.

_Tone: knowing, relatable._

### Beat 4: What the puzzle really shows (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, some researchers say the 2-4-6 task shows something slightly different: a habit of testing positive examples, which is often a sensible shortcut in daily life.
Wason himself called it confirmation bias, and the name stuck.
(pause) Either way, the trap is the same: if you only ask questions that can say "yes", you'll never hear "no".

**[VISUAL]** Two scholar silhouettes at a whiteboard: one writes CONFIRMATION BIAS, the other POSITIVE TESTING. A balance sits between them. Then a question box that only has a YES button; the NO button is missing.

_Tone: fair, thoughtful; firm on the last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's the habit that protects you. Before you decide you're right, try to prove yourself wrong. Scientists have a name for this: trying to falsify your idea.
Ask: "What would I expect to see if I were mistaken?" Then go and look for that. Read one strong argument from the other side.
(pause) A belief that survives a real test is worth keeping. One that's never been tested is just a guess.

**[VISUAL]** The figure takes off the MY BELIEF glasses. They walk down the overgrown "could say NO" path and test 10, 7, 1. A small ✗ appears, and the figure smiles as they update their notebook. Final card: TESTED BELIEF ✓ · UNTESTED = GUESS.

_Tone: warm, authoritative (peak); balanced last line._
