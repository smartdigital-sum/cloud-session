**Text**
```
[softly] Ten people praise your post. One person leaves a nasty comment.
[short pause] Which one are you still thinking about tonight?

[curious] Have you ever noticed how one criticism can wipe out a whole day of compliments? Or how bad news grabs you faster than good news?
[reassuring] You're not being dramatic. Psychologists call it negativity bias: bad things weigh more in the mind than good things of the same size.
[short pause] And there may be a very old reason for it.

[thoughtful] In 2001, psychologist Roy Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed a huge range of research: on relationships, feedback, memory, health and more.
Their conclusion was right there in the title: "Bad is stronger than good."
Bad feedback tends to hit harder than good feedback. Bad moments are remembered more vividly. One bad event can outweigh several good ones. That's why one bad review can feel louder than fifty good ones.
[short pause] Not always. But again and again.

[intrigued] Psychologist Paul Rozin put it in one unforgettable image.
A single cockroach in a bowl of cherries ruins the whole bowl. But a single cherry in a bowl of cockroaches does nothing to make it appetising. It's why one rude remark can colour how we see a whole person.
[short pause] A little bad contaminates a lot of good. The reverse rarely works.

[thoughtful] And this isn't just one culture. In 2019, researchers measured the bodies of over a thousand people across seventeen countries, on six continents, as they watched news stories.
On average, negative stories triggered stronger physical reactions, like changes in skin sweat and pulse.
But here's the hopeful part: people varied a lot, and some reacted more to positive news.
[short pause] Negativity grabs us. But it doesn't own us.

[mysterious] So why would a brain be built this way? One leading explanation is survival.
For our ancestors, missing a meal was bad. Missing a snake could be the end. A mind that paid extra attention to threats was more likely to survive.
Think of a smoke alarm: better a hundred false alarms than one missed fire.
[short pause] That alarm system kept us alive. But today, it goes off for comment sections, notifications and headlines.

[warmly] So how do you live with it? First, notice it: when one comment ruins your day, say, "That's negativity bias." Naming it shrinks it.
Second, balance the scale on purpose: keep the kind messages, and reread them. When something good happens, pause for a few seconds and really let it land.
Third, ask: "Is this one thing the whole story?" Usually, it isn't. And choose your news; don't just absorb it.
[long pause] Bad is stronger than good. So give good a little help.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what's one compliment you still remember years later? Let's fill this comment section with good ones.
And subscribe. Next, the finale: five famous psychology experiments that fell apart, and the ones that still stand.
```
