## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever noticed how one criticism can wipe out a whole day of compliments? Or how bad news grabs you faster than good news?
You're not being dramatic. Psychologists call it negativity bias: bad things weigh more in the mind than good things of the same size.
(pause) And there may be a very old reason for it.

**[VISUAL]** A balance scale: a stack of ten small golden hearts on one side, one dark stone on the other; the stone side slams down. Title card: **NEGATIVITY BIAS**. A faint cave-painting texture flickers at the edge.

_Tone: curious, reassuring on "You're not being dramatic"; mysterious last line._

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: "Bad is stronger than good" (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 2001, psychologist Roy Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed a huge range of research: on relationships, feedback, memory, health and more.
Their conclusion was right there in the title: "Bad is stronger than good."
Bad feedback tends to hit harder than good feedback. Bad moments are remembered more vividly. One bad event can outweigh several good ones. That's why one bad review can feel louder than fifty good ones.
(pause) Not always. But again and again.

**[VISUAL]** A journal cover with the title BAD IS STRONGER THAN GOOD. Four quick panels: a feedback form with one red line circled, a memory album where one dark photo is larger than the rest, a calendar where one storm cloud covers several suns, a 4.8-star shop page where the eye goes straight to the single 1-star review. Caption: _Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs, 2001_.

_Tone: thoughtful, authoritative; measured last line._

### Beat 2: The cockroach and the cherries (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Psychologist Paul Rozin put it in one unforgettable image.
A single cockroach in a bowl of cherries ruins the whole bowl. But a single cherry in a bowl of cockroaches does nothing to make it appetising. It's why one rude remark can colour how we see a whole person.
(pause) A little bad contaminates a lot of good. The reverse rarely works.

**[VISUAL]** A beautiful bowl of glossy red cherries. A cartoon cockroach drops in; the whole bowl greys out. Reverse: a (stylised, not gross) bowl of cockroaches with one cherry on top; nothing changes. Caption: _Rozin & Royzman, 2001_.

_Tone: intrigued, slightly playful; crisp last line._

### Beat 3: Seventeen countries, one pattern (mostly) (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
And this isn't just one culture. In 2019, researchers measured the bodies of over a thousand people across seventeen countries, on six continents, as they watched news stories.
On average, negative stories triggered stronger physical reactions, like changes in skin sweat and pulse.
But here's the hopeful part: people varied a lot, and some reacted more to positive news.
(pause) Negativity grabs us. But it doesn't own us.

**[VISUAL]** A world map; 17 countries light up across six continents. Viewers in different homes watch a laptop with finger sensors attached; small sensor graphs spike higher for a "BAD NEWS" clip. Then a few viewers' graphs spike for "GOOD NEWS" instead, glowing gold. Caption: _Soroka, Fournier & Nir, 2019_.

_Tone: thoughtful; hopeful lift; firm last line._

### Beat 4: A very old alarm system (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So why would a brain be built this way? One leading explanation is survival.
For our ancestors, missing a meal was bad. Missing a snake could be the end. A mind that paid extra attention to threats was more likely to survive.
Think of a smoke alarm: better a hundred false alarms than one missed fire.
(pause) That alarm system kept us alive. But today, it goes off for comment sections, notifications and headlines.

**[VISUAL]** Ancient grassland at dusk: an early human notices a snake in the grass and steps back; berries nearby are ignored. A smoke alarm on a ceiling beeps at burnt toast. Cut to today: the same alarm icon flashes over a phone's comment section, notification badges and a red news ticker.

_Tone: mysterious storyteller → modern, a little wry._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So how do you live with it? First, notice it: when one comment ruins your day, say, "That's negativity bias." Naming it shrinks it.
Second, balance the scale on purpose: keep the kind messages, and reread them. When something good happens, pause for a few seconds and really let it land.
Third, ask: "Is this one thing the whole story?" Usually, it isn't. And choose your news; don't just absorb it.
(pause) Bad is stronger than good. So give good a little help.

**[VISUAL]** A label sticker NEGATIVITY BIAS slaps onto the red comment; it shrinks. A "Kind words" folder fills with screenshots of warm messages. A zoom-out from the single red comment to the full page of kind ones. The balance scale levels as the figure adds more golden hearts by hand.

_Tone: warm, encouraging (peak); gentle last line._
