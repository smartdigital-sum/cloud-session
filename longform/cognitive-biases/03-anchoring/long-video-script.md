## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The rigged wheel of fortune (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1974, psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman ran a strange experiment. They spun a wheel of fortune, secretly rigged to stop on either 10 or 65.
Then they asked people: is the percentage of African countries in the United Nations higher or lower than that number? And what's your best guess?
People who saw 10 guessed, on average, about 25 percent. People who saw 65 guessed about 45.
(pause) A spinning wheel. A twenty-point swing.

**[VISUAL]** The wheel, now seen from behind: a hidden magnet ensures it stops at 10 or 65. Two groups of figures with answer cards: 10 → ~25%, 65 → ~45%. A gap bar of 20 points glows between them. Caption: _Tversky & Kahneman, 1974_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; slow on the last two short sentences._

### BODY 2: Judges and loaded dice (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
You might think experts are immune. In 2006, German researchers gave experienced judges a realistic shoplifting case.
Before sentencing, the judges rolled dice that were secretly loaded to land on a low or a high number.
Judges who rolled the higher number gave longer sentences. These weren't students guessing trivia. These were people whose job is to weigh evidence.
(pause) The dice had nothing to do with the crime. The judges knew it. And it still mattered.

**[VISUAL]** A courtroom in silhouette; a judge's hand rolls two dice across the bench. Close-up: the dice land on a low or high total. A gavel falls; a calendar of sentence months stretches longer for the high roll (illustrative, no exact numbers). Caption: _Englich, Mussweiler & Strack, 2006_.

_Tone: serious; three slow beats at the end._

### BODY 3: Why anchors work (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Why does this happen? One explanation: when you're unsure, you start from the number in front of you and adjust, but you stop adjusting too early. It's like starting a walk from the wrong door: you move, but you never get far enough away.
Another: the anchor quietly makes your mind search for reasons that fit it. If someone says ₹1,999, your brain starts noticing everything that makes the item seem worth a lot.
(pause) Either way, the first number becomes the centre of gravity.

**[VISUAL]** Two animations. ① A slider starts at the anchor and moves toward the true value but stops halfway, labelled STOPS TOO EARLY. ② A spotlight sweeps over a jacket and lights up only its "premium" details (stitching, logo, fabric) once ₹1,999 is said. Final image: numbers orbiting the anchor like planets around a sun.

_Tone: thoughtful, clear._

### BODY 4: Anchors everywhere, and a finding that holds up (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
And it's everywhere. The MRP printed above a "sale" price. The first offer in a salary negotiation. The "was ₹4,999" crossed out in red. Bazaar bargaining, where the first price sets the whole game.
And unlike some famous findings, this one holds up. In a large 2014 project that re-tested classic effects across dozens of labs, anchoring came back strong.
(pause) This bias is real.

**[VISUAL]** Quick montage: an MRP sticker, a salary offer letter with the first number highlighted, a red strikethrough price, two people haggling over vegetables. Then a world map with 36 lab pins lighting up one by one, each stamping **REPLICATED**. Caption: _Many Labs 1, Klein et al., 2014_.

_Tone: knowing, then firm and confident on "This bias is real."_

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So how do you fight a number you can't un-hear? Make your own anchor first.
Before you shop, decide what something is worth to you. Before a negotiation, write down your number. And when you see a big crossed-out price, ask: "Would I pay this if I'd never seen that number?"
(pause) The first number isn't a fact. It's a move.

**[VISUAL]** The viewer-figure writes their own price on a sticky note before entering a shop. A notebook shows "MY NUMBER: ₹___". The crossed-out MRP tag fades to blank, leaving only the real price. A chess piece slides across a board: THE FIRST NUMBER = A MOVE.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); crisp last line._
