## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The tomato (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
The student was Francesco Cirillo. In his own words: "I couldn't focus on my university studies."
So he made a deal with himself. Twist the timer. Work on one thing until it rings. Nothing else.
In Italian, tomato is "pomodoro". So each burst of work became a pomodoro.
Over time, it settled into a simple rhythm: twenty-five minutes of work, a five-minute break, and a longer break after four rounds.
Today there are books, apps, and countless study-with-me videos built on it. (pause) All from a tomato.

**[VISUAL]** Name card: FRANCESCO CIRILLO (illustrated, not a photo). The quote types onto a notebook page. A dictionary card: **POMODORO = TOMATO 🍅**. A circular rhythm diagram fills in: four red 25-min arcs, small green 5-min gaps, one big green arc after the fourth. Montage of modern timers, ending on the original tomato.

_Tone: warm storytelling; affectionate on "All from a tomato."_

### Beat 2: Why it works (you already know) (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
If you've watched this series, you already know why it works.
A timer shrinks the task. Not "finish the project". Just "one pomodoro". And as we saw, a smaller task means a smaller bad feeling.
A timer is a plan with a clear end. Your brain can relax, because it knows when this stops.
And while it's ticking, there's one rule: one thing only. No switching.
(pause) The tomato quietly fixes three problems at once.

**[VISUAL]** Three callback cards slide in, each with a small icon from earlier videos: **SHRINK IT** (02's boulder → pebble), **A CLEAR END** (01's loop closing), **NO SWITCHING** (04's track switch locked). The tomato timer sits in the middle as all three light up.

_Tone: knowing, friendly; satisfying "three problems at once."_

### Beat 3: The secret is the break (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
But the cleverest part isn't the work. It's the break.
In 2011, psychologists at the University of Illinois tested a long, boring focus task, the kind where attention slowly slips.
People who worked straight through got worse over time. But people who briefly switched to something else, just twice… didn't drop.
Wait, didn't we say switching costs you? It does. But this is different: a planned pause, not constant jumping.
The researchers argued that the brain starts to tune out anything constant. (pause) A short break makes the task feel new again.

**[VISUAL]** Two lines on a simple graph across a 50-minute bar: one slopes down (STRAIGHT THROUGH), one stays level with two small blips (2 SHORT SWITCHES). Caption: _Ariga & Lleras, 2011_. Split card: ❌ constant jumping (04's chaotic spotlight) vs ✅ planned pause (a calm pause icon). A ceiling fan hums; its sound wave fades to silence, then a break chime brings the room back into colour.

_Tone: curious; a playful "Wait…" beat; clear on the difference._

### Beat 4: Does science back Pomodoro? (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So does the Pomodoro method itself work? In 2023, researchers at Maastricht University in the Netherlands tested it with eighty-seven students.
Some took breaks whenever they felt like it. Others took fixed breaks on a timer.
The self-timers worked in longer stretches, and took longer breaks. They also felt more tired, more distracted, and less motivated.
The timer groups got a similar amount done… in less time.
But honesty, as always: it was a small, one-day study, and one study is never the final word. (pause) And even it didn't use exactly twenty-five minutes.

**[VISUAL]** Three groups of student figures at laptops: FREE BREAKS · 24 + 6 · 12 + 3. Mood meters: the free-break group dips (tired, distracted); the timer groups stay steady. Clocks show the timer groups finishing earlier. Caption: _Biwer et al., 2023_. A small "24 ≠ 25" sticker pops onto the timer.

_Tone: thoughtful and fair; a light smile on the last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's the truth about the tomato. The magic isn't twenty-five minutes. It's the deal.
One thing. A clear end. A real break.
Start with twenty-five if focus feels hard. Stretch it longer when you're in deep work.
And make the break a real one: stand up, look out of a window, drink water. Not your phone. (pause) Your phone isn't a break. It's another task.

**[VISUAL]** The tomato opens like a locket to reveal three words: **ONE THING · CLEAR END · REAL BREAK**. A slider stretches the red arc from 25 to 50. Break montage: stretching, a window view, a glass of water. A hand reaches for the phone; the phone gets a red "ANOTHER TASK" label and is set down.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); a firm, kind last line._
