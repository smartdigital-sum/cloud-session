## MAIN BODY (COUNTDOWN)

### BODY 1: #5 The highlighter (0:40–1:05)

**[NARRATION]**
Number five: the highlighter.
It feels like studying. The page turns yellow, and you feel progress.
But in 2013, a team of psychologists led by John Dunlosky reviewed a huge body of research on ten popular study techniques. Highlighting and rereading were rated low in usefulness.
The two that came out on top? Testing yourself, and spreading study across days.
Instead: close the book, and try to recall what you just read.

**[VISUAL]** A textbook page slowly turning entirely yellow; a "PROGRESS?" meter fills, then flickers. A ranking board of 10 techniques: HIGHLIGHTING and REREADING in a grey LOW band; PRACTICE TESTING and SPACED PRACTICE in a gold HIGH band. Caption: _Dunlosky et al., 2013_. A hand closes the book; a thought bubble fills with recalled points.

_Tone: light, slightly teasing on "the page turns yellow"; clear on the fix._

### BODY 2: #4 The study playlist with lyrics (1:05–1:35)

**[NARRATION]**
Number four: the study playlist with lyrics.
In 2014, researchers at Cardiff Metropolitan University in Wales gave people a reading test in silence, with instrumental music, or with songs that had lyrics.
Silence did best. Songs with lyrics did worst, even songs people liked.
Your brain tries to process those words, while you're trying to read other words.
Instead: silence, or music without lyrics, for anything that needs reading or writing.

**[VISUAL]** Three study booths: SILENCE (quiet, focused), INSTRUMENTAL (soft notes), LYRICS (speech bubbles of song words floating over the page and tangling with the text). Simple bars: SILENCE highest, LYRICS lowest, with a small "even favourite songs" tag. Caption: _Perham & Currie, 2014_.

_Tone: curious, clear; a gentle "aha" on the brain line._

### BODY 3: #3 Visualising success (1:35–2:05)

**[NARRATION]**
Number three: just visualise your success.
In 2011, psychologists at New York University found something surprising. When people fantasised about an ideal future, their energy dropped. Even their blood pressure dipped.
The dream felt so good, the brain relaxed, as if part of the job was already done.
Instead: picture the goal, then picture the obstacle in your way. And make a plan for it.

**[VISUAL]** A dreamy vision board glows: a trophy, a graduation cap, a beach. A small energy battery beside the dreamer drains; a blood-pressure gauge needle eases down. Then the board flips: the goal on one side, a rock labelled OBSTACLE on the path, and a small plan card bridging them. Caption: _Kappes & Oettingen, 2011_.

_Tone: thoughtful; soft and dreamy on the fantasy, then grounded on "Instead."_

### BODY 4: #2 Announcing your goals (2:05–2:35)

**[NARRATION]**
Number two: telling everyone your goal.
In 2009, psychologist Peter Gollwitzer and colleagues found that when people announced identity goals, like "I'm going to be a great lawyer", and others took notice, they then worked on them less.
Being seen as that person gave them a premature sense of already being it.
It's one set of studies, so don't treat it as law. But it's worth a thought.
Instead: keep the goal quiet until you've started. Or share your progress, not your plans.

**[VISUAL]** A phone posts "Future lawyer! ⚖️"; likes and hearts pour in; a little "✓ DONE?" badge appears on the person's chest. Their study timer then stops early. Caption: _Gollwitzer et al., 2009_. A small "ONE SET OF STUDIES" label. Final card: ~~PLANS~~ → PROGRESS.

_Tone: knowing, fair; careful on the caveat._

### BODY 5: #1 Sleeping less (2:35–3:05)

**[NARRATION]**
And number one, the hack that costs the most: sleeping less to get more done.
In a 2003 study, people slept six hours a night for two weeks. By the end, their attention was as impaired as people who'd gone up to two nights without any sleep at all.
But here's the scary part: they barely felt sleepy. They didn't notice how much they'd slipped.
Every hour you steal from sleep, you pay back in focus. (pause) With interest.

**[VISUAL]** A calendar flips through 14 nights, each with a 6-hour sleep bar. An attention meter slowly sinks night after night until it matches a second figure who hasn't slept for two nights. A thought bubble over the 6-hour sleeper says "I'm fine 👍" while the meter blinks red. Caption: _Van Dongen et al., 2003_. A receipt prints: SLEEP DEBT, PAID WITH FOCUS.

_Tone: serious, the most weight in the video; slow on "With interest."_

So notice the pattern. Every one of these hacks feels productive. That's the trap.
Real focus often feels less exciting: testing yourself, silence, planning for obstacles, quiet progress, and sleep.
(pause) If something only feels productive, check it. If it actually works, keep it.
