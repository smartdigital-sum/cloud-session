## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The fresh start effect (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 2014, researchers at the Wharton School studied years of Google searches for one word: "diet." Searches rose at the start of every new year, every new month… and every new week.
They saw the same pattern at a university gym. More visits at the start of a week, a month, a semester.
They called it the fresh start effect. A new beginning feels like a line between the old you… and a new one.
That's real, and useful. (pause) But here's the catch. The "new you" writes the plan… and the old you has to live it.

**[VISUAL]** A search bar types "diet"; a line graph shows small bumps every Monday and big spikes each January (illustrative, no real values). A gym turnstile spins faster on Mondays. A chalk line is drawn across the floor: OLD YOU | NEW YOU. The "new you" hands a giant plan back across the line to the old one. Caption: _Dai, Milkman & Riis, 2014_.

_Tone: curious and light; a wry smile on "the old you has to live it."_

### BODY 2: The planning fallacy (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1979, psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the second force: the planning fallacy. We underestimate how long our own tasks will take, even when we've been wrong before.
In a 1994 study, students guessed how long their final-year thesis would take. On average: about thirty-four days.
The real average? About fifty-six.
Only around a third finished by the date they predicted. (pause) These were smart, motivated people. Planning their own work.

**[VISUAL]** The shadowy force reveals its label: PLANNING FALLACY. Two calendar bars grow side by side: GUESS 34 DAYS vs REAL 56 DAYS. A row of student figures; only about one in three gets a green tick at their deadline. Caption: _Buehler, Griffin & Ross, 1994_.

_Tone: thoughtful; let the gap between 34 and 56 land with a pause._

### BODY 3: Even experts (1:50–2:20)

**[NARRATION]**
And it's not just students.
When work began on the Sydney Opera House, it was planned to take about four years and cost seven million Australian dollars.
It opened in 1973. A decade late. At a cost of around a hundred and two million: about fourteen times the estimate.
The writer Douglas Hofstadter even turned this into a law: "It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law."

**[VISUAL]** 2D Sydney harbour. The Opera House sails build slowly while a calendar flips through the 1960s; a price tag balloons from A$7M to A$102M. The quote types onto a paper card, its last words looping back to its start like a spiral.

_Tone: serious, then a gentle laugh on the Hofstadter quote._

So why does every plan break, even the experts'? (pause) The answer is hiding in the space you didn't leave.

### BODY 4: Why every plan breaks (2:20–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So why do we keep doing this?
Because when you plan, you imagine the task going right. You picture the work… not the interruptions.
Monday's plan has no room for a late reply, a sick day, or a task that turns out twice as big.
Kahneman called this the "inside view": looking only at this task, instead of asking how tasks like it really went before.
Then Wednesday arrives. Real life shows up. The plan breaks… and it feels like *you* failed.
(pause) And as we saw last time, that bad feeling is exactly what makes you quit.

**[VISUAL]** The planner as a perfect grid with zero gaps. Interruptions fly in like birds (a phone call, a fever thermometer, a "quick question" bubble, a task that doubles in size) and knock blocks out of the grid. The figure looks at the wreck; the grey storm cloud from video 02 drifts in.

_Tone: empathetic, building; slow and quiet on "you failed."_

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So don't plan for Monday you. Plan for Wednesday you.
One: look back, not forward. Before you guess, ask: how long did this *actually* take last time?
Two: break it into parts. In studies, listing every step made people's time estimates longer, and less over-optimistic.
Three: leave empty space. Not every hour needs a task. The gaps are where real life fits.
A plan that bends… (pause) is a plan that survives.

**[VISUAL]** Three cards flip: **1 LOOK BACK · 2 BREAK IT DOWN · 3 LEAVE SPACE**. The planner is rebuilt: fewer blocks, a past-week reference on the side, one big block split into small steps, and clear white gaps. Interruptions fly in again, land in the gaps, and nothing breaks.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); slow on the last line._
