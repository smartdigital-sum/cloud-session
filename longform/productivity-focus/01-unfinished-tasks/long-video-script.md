## HOOK (0:00–0:10)

**[NARRATION]**
The tasks you haven't finished are stealing your sleep and your focus.
It's midnight. Your body is done… and your mind is still replaying the message you never answered.

## LOCK-IN (0:10–0:30)

**[NARRATION]**
Psychologists have chased this for almost a hundred years.
It started with a waiter who never wrote anything down. Then a famous experiment. Then, in 2025, a twist that most videos about it still get wrong.
And at the end, I'll give you a five-minute fix you can try tonight. (pause) It isn't finishing the task.

**[VISUAL]** The sticky notes freeze. Three sealed cards slide in: **THE WAITER · THE EXPERIMENT · THE TWIST**. A fourth glowing card, **THE FIX**, drops in and stays sealed. _Tone: intrigued, building; three quick beats on the three cards; slow and firm on "It isn't finishing the task."_

## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The waiter (0:30–1:05)

**[NARRATION]**
As the story goes, a group of psychologists sat in a café. Among them: Kurt Lewin… and his student, Bluma Zeigarnik.
They noticed something strange. Their waiter remembered every order perfectly, with no notepad, as long as the bill was still unpaid.
But the moment the table paid? (pause) The order vanished from his memory. Completely.
Same waiter. Same brain. The only difference was this: one task was *open*… and one was *closed*.
Zeigarnik wanted to know: is this true for everyone?

**[VISUAL]** 2D café scene. A waiter with glowing thought bubbles of each table's order. Coins land on one table: its bubble pops. Split-screen: an "OPEN" padlock glowing vs a "CLOSED" padlock gone grey. Name captions for Lewin and Zeigarnik (illustrated, not photos).

_Tone: playful curiosity on the café story; a beat of silence before "Completely."_

### BODY 2: The experiment (1:05–1:35)

**[NARRATION]**
So she ran an experiment. People were given a series of small tasks: puzzles, sums, little clay figures.
Some tasks they were allowed to finish. Others, she stopped halfway.
Later, she asked them to recall the tasks.
The interrupted ones came back far more often. In her results, nearly *twice* as often as the finished ones.
She published it in 1927. Today, we call it the Zeigarnik effect.
(pause) But here's the part most videos skip.

**[VISUAL]** A desk with task cards; a hand flips half of them over mid-task (a red "STOP" stamp). Memory bubbles: interrupted cards float up more often than finished ones; a simple 2-bar chart labelled "remembered" (no exact number on screen). Stamp: **ZEIGARNIK EFFECT · 1927**. Last line: screen glitches, record-scratch.

_Tone: steady, explanatory; a knowing smile on "the part most videos skip."_

### REHOOK 1 (1:35): "But here's the part most videos skip" (last line of Body 2)

### BODY 3: What most videos get wrong (1:35–2:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 2025, researchers gathered nearly a century of these studies and checked them all again.
The memory part? (pause) It didn't hold up reliably. Unfinished tasks are not always easier to remember.
But something else *did* hold up. Something even more powerful.
People have a strong urge to go back and finish what they started. Another of Lewin's students, Maria Ovsiankina, spotted this back in 1928.
So your mind may not remember every open task… but it keeps *pulling* you back to them.

**[VISUAL]** A stack of research papers spanning 1927 → 2025 on a timeline. The "memory" bar shrinks with a "?" stamp. A new label rises: **THE URGE TO FINISH**. A half-built clay figure; a hand keeps drifting back toward it like a magnet. Caption: **OVSIANKINA EFFECT · 1928**.

_Tone: honest, slightly surprised on "didn't hold up"; build weight on "even more powerful."_

### REHOOK 2 (2:15): "And that pull has a price"

### BODY 4: The hidden cost (2:15–2:55)

**[NARRATION]**
And that pull has a price.
In 2011, psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister asked people to think about an important task they hadn't finished. Then they gave them something completely different to do.
Their minds kept drifting back to the unfinished task. And they did worse on simple word puzzles.
That's what an open loop does. It runs in the background, like an app you forgot to close, quietly draining your battery.
Now imagine every half-done thing in your life… running at the same time.
(pause) But the same study found the way out.

**[VISUAL]** A person reading; thought bubbles of the unfinished task keep interrupting the page. Anagram tiles scramble and won't settle. Cut to a phone screen with dozens of open apps; the battery icon drains red. Pull back: the person's head full of glowing open loops. Last line: one loop gently closes with a click and light.

_Tone: serious and grounded; slow down on "quietly draining your battery"; hopeful lift on the last line._

## CLOSING: The fix (2:55–3:40)

_Pays off the lock-in: the fix isn't finishing the task._

**[NARRATION]**
The people who escaped it had not finished their task.
They did one small thing: they made a specific plan. *What* they would do, *when*, and *where*.
That was enough. The intrusive thoughts faded.
Your brain doesn't need the task done. (pause) It needs to *trust* that it will be done.
A clear plan is a promise your mind believes.

**[VISUAL]** A notebook opens; three lines write themselves: **WHAT · WHEN · WHERE**. As each is written, one glowing loop around the head closes and dims. Final frame: a calm face, the orbiting notes landing neatly on a page.

_Tone: the emotional peak, warm and certain; long pause before "trust"; land the last line slowly._

### Tonight (closing, part 2)

**[NARRATION]**
So tonight, try this. Before bed, take five minutes and write tomorrow's to-do list. Be specific.
In one sleep study, people who did this fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks they'd already finished.
Close the loop on paper… so your mind can finally let go.

## CTA (3:40–3:55)

**[NARRATION]**
Tell me in the comments: what's the one unfinished task living in your head right now? (pause) And next, we find out why we put these tasks off in the first place.

**[VISUAL]** Back in the opening bedroom, now calm. A hand writes a short list by lamplight; the lamp clicks off; the figure falls asleep. End screen: comment prompt "What's YOUR open loop?" + next-video card **Procrastination Isn't Laziness**.

_Tone: gentle, practical; a warm smile on the comment question; quiet confidence on the last line._
