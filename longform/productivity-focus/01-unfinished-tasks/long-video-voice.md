**How to generate a 4-minute track**

2. Try all 8 Text blocks together in one run first. Keep the blank lines between sections, since they give natural pauses.
3. If the voice drifts, speeds up or cuts off near the end, generate **one section per run** with the same voice and style, then join the clips in your editor (add ~0.5s silence between sections).

## English

**Style instructions**

**Text**
```
[firmly] The tasks you haven't finished are stealing your sleep and your focus.
[quieter] It's midnight. Your body is done… and your mind is still replaying the message you never answered.

[intrigued] Psychologists have chased this for almost a hundred years.
It started with a waiter who never wrote anything down. Then a famous experiment. Then, in 2025, a twist that most videos about it still get wrong.
And at the end, I'll give you a five-minute fix you can try tonight. [short pause] [firmly] It isn't finishing the task.

[curious] As the story goes, a group of psychologists sat in a café. Among them: Kurt Lewin… and his student, Bluma Zeigarnik.
They noticed something strange. Their waiter remembered every order perfectly, with no notepad, as long as the bill was still unpaid.
But the moment the table paid? [short pause] The order vanished from his memory. Completely.
Same waiter. Same brain. The only difference was this: one task was open… and one was closed.
Zeigarnik wanted to know: is this true for everyone?

[calm] So she ran an experiment. People were given a series of small tasks: puzzles, sums, little clay figures.
Some tasks they were allowed to finish. Others, she stopped halfway.
Later, she asked them to recall the tasks.
The interrupted ones came back far more often. In her results, nearly twice as often as the finished ones.
She published it in 1927. Today, we call it the Zeigarnik effect.
[short pause] But here's the part most videos skip.

[thoughtful] In 2025, researchers gathered nearly a century of these studies and checked them all again.
The memory part? [short pause] It didn't hold up reliably. Unfinished tasks are not always easier to remember.
But something else did hold up. Something even more powerful.
People have a strong urge to go back and finish what they started. Another of Lewin's students, Maria Ovsiankina, spotted this back in 1928.
So your mind may not remember every open task… but it keeps pulling you back to them.

[serious] And that pull has a price.
In 2011, psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister asked people to think about an important task they hadn't finished. Then they gave them something completely different to do.
Their minds kept drifting back to the unfinished task. And they did worse on simple word puzzles.
That's what an open loop does. It runs in the background, like an app you forgot to close, quietly draining your battery.
Now imagine every half-done thing in your life… running at the same time.
[short pause] But the same study found the way out.

[warmly] The people who escaped it had not finished their task.
They did one small thing: they made a specific plan. What they would do, when, and where.
That was enough. The intrusive thoughts faded.
Your brain doesn't need the task done. [long pause] It needs to trust that it will be done.
A clear plan is a promise your mind believes.

[gently] So tonight, try this. Before bed, take five minutes and write tomorrow's to-do list. Be specific.
In one sleep study, people who did this fell asleep faster than people who wrote about tasks they'd already finished.
Close the loop on paper… so your mind can finally let go.
[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what's the one unfinished task living in your head right now? [short pause] And next, we find out why we put these tasks off in the first place.
```
