# 05 · Your Phone Steals Focus Even When It's Silent

Rewritten using the *Short-Form Storytelling* structure: Hook → Lock-In → Body 1 → Rehook → Body 2 → Rehook → Body 3 → Closing → CTA.
**Niche:** 1.2 Self-improvement: Productivity & Focus · **Format:** deep-dive, 16:9 faceless (2D animation)
**Narration style:** calm, deep, wise mentor; second person; short lines; firm opening, then deliberate pauses; empowering close
**Voiceover:** English, Hindi, Assamese → [long-video-voice.md](long-video-voice.md) · **Reel version:** [short-reel-script.md](short-reel-script.md)
**Series:** final part of the focus series (01–05), follows [04 Multitasking](../04-multitasking/long-video-script.md). Outro opens a new chapter with idea #5 (the tomato timer).

## What changed from the first draft, and why

| Problem in the first draft | Fix (guide rule) |
|---|---|
| The hook was whispered and slow, and its stakes were vague ("you might not notice… until it's gone"). | **Four S's:** subject + stakes in sentence one ("does not protect your focus"), firm delivery. |
| 0:15–0:40 was a series recap ("We've looked at open loops…"). New viewers have no reason to care, and it's where they leave. | Replaced by a **lock-in zone**: a magic box (three quiet pulls), each pull teased in one line, and the payoff promised ("one move that beats all three, and it isn't willpower"). The series recap moves to the outro, where it rewards fans instead of losing new viewers. |
| "But this is where honesty matters" was a pause with no pull forward. | The replication result is now part of Body 1's **contrarian insight** ("what the headlines skipped"). |
| The transitions into Pull 2 and Pull 3 were labels ("Pull two: …"). | Real **rehooks** that open a new curiosity loop at ~1:40 and ~2:25, resetting the attention window. |
| The outro had three asks (comment, subscribe, next video). | One clear **CTA**, after a short closing that pays off the hook. |

## HOOK (0:00–0:10)

**[NARRATION]**
Putting your phone on silent, face down, does not protect your focus.
Part of your brain is still holding onto it. (pause) And you can't feel it happening.

**[VISUAL]** Frame 1 is already moving: a hand slaps a phone face down on a desk next to an open notebook (thud). A thin glowing thread snaps taut from the phone to the figure's head. The figure goes back to work; the thread pulses gently. Slow push-in on the phone.

_Tone: firm and close; not whispered. Quieter on "you can't feel it happening."_

## LOCK-IN (0:10–0:30)

**[NARRATION]**
Researchers have studied this for more than a decade. And they've found three quiet ways your phone pulls on your attention, without you ever touching it.
The first is famous, and scientists still argue about it. The second, you've lived through this week. The third, your own brain makes up.
And at the end, I'll show you the one simple move that beats all three. (pause) It isn't willpower.

**[VISUAL]** Title card: **3 QUIET PULLS**. Three sealed envelopes slide in, labelled 1 · 2 · 3. Each gets a one-word stamp as it's named: **DISPUTED** · **FELT** · **INVENTED**. A fourth, glowing envelope labelled **THE FIX** drops in and stays sealed. A "WILLPOWER" badge is crossed out on "It isn't willpower."

_Tone: intrigued, building; a small smile on "your own brain makes up"; firm and slow on the last line._

## BODY 1: The "brain drain" study (0:30–1:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Pull one. In 2017, researchers at the University of Texas at Austin ran a simple test. Hundreds of people did tasks that measure working memory and problem solving. Every phone was on silent.
The only difference was where the phone was. On the desk, face down. In a pocket or bag. Or in another room.
The people whose phone was in another room did best. The ones with it on the desk did worst.
And here's the strange part: most of them said they weren't thinking about their phone at all.
The researchers called it "brain drain". (pause) But here's what the headlines skipped.
When another team repeated the experiment in 2022, they found… no difference.
A 2023 review of more than twenty studies did find an overall cost from having a phone nearby. But the size of it varied a lot.
So the fair answer is: the effect is probably real, but likely smaller than you've heard.

**[VISUAL]** Three identical desks side by side. Desk A: phone face down. Desk B: phone peeking out of a bag. Desk C: phone shown in a separate room through a doorway. A simple "brainpower" bar above each person: C fullest, A lowest (illustrative, no exact values). A thought bubble over Desk A's person: "Phone? What phone?" Caption: _Ward et al., 2017_. A newspaper headline slams in: **"YOUR PHONE IS DRAINING YOUR BRAIN"**. On "what the headlines skipped", the headline tears. Second lab with three desks: all bars equal, stamp **NO DIFFERENCE (2022)**. Then a stack of 20+ paper icons, scale tipping slightly toward "COST", with a wobbly needle. Final card: **PROBABLY REAL · PROBABLY SMALLER**.

_Tone: curious and clear; light surprise on "the strange part"; a knowing turn on "what the headlines skipped"; honest and measured, no drama, on the replication._

## REHOOK 1 (1:40)

**[NARRATION]**
(pause) The next pull doesn't need a lab to prove it, because you've felt it this week.

**[VISUAL]** The lab scene slides away; hard cut to the figure's own desk. The phone lights up.

_Tone: a lift; leaning in._

## BODY 2: The buzz you don't answer (1:42–2:20)

**[NARRATION]**
In 2015, researchers at Florida State University gave people a task that needs steady attention. During it, some of them got a call or text notification on their own phone. They didn't pick it up.
They still made more mistakes. And the drop was similar in size to what earlier studies found when people actually used their phones.
Why? Because one buzz opens a loop: "Who was that?" (pause) And as we saw in the first video, open loops don't stay quiet.

**[VISUAL]** A person doing a fast-paced task (numbers flashing on screen, tapping a key). The phone on the desk lights up with a buzz; the person doesn't touch it, but a small "WHO WAS THAT?" bubble appears and the next few taps turn red. Callback: the glowing open-loop ring from video 01 forms around the bubble. Caption: _Stothart et al., 2015_.

_Tone: curious; gentle callback on the last line._

## REHOOK 2 (2:20)

**[NARRATION]**
And the third pull is the strangest of all, because the buzz doesn't even have to be real.

**[VISUAL]** Freeze-frame on the figure. Their pocket glows… then the glow fades, and the pocket is empty.

_Tone: playful, a little conspiratorial._

## BODY 3: Phantom vibrations (2:24–2:55)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever felt your phone vibrate… and it hadn't?
In one study, nearly nine in ten university students said it had happened to them. In a hospital survey, about two in three doctors and staff.
It's mostly harmless. But it shows something: part of your attention is always on standby. Waiting for the next buzz.

**[VISUAL]** A figure pats their pocket, pulls out the phone: blank screen, no notifications. A puzzled "?" pops up. Two simple icon rows: 9 of 10 students highlighted; 2 of 3 hospital staff highlighted. A small radar dish on the figure's head slowly sweeps, labelled STANDBY.

_Tone: playful on the question, then thoughtful on "standby."_

## CLOSING: The fix (2:55–3:25)

**[NARRATION]**
So what beats all three? Not more willpower. Distance.
For your most important hour of the day, put your phone in another room. Not face down. Not in your pocket. Gone.
Turn off every notification that isn't from a real person. Check messages at set times, the way we batched them last video.
You don't need to resist something that isn't there. (pause) Distance does the work for you.

**[VISUAL]** The glowing **THE FIX** envelope from the lock-in opens: **DISTANCE**. The figure stands, walks the phone to another room, closes the door. Back at the desk, the glowing thread from the hook snaps and fades; the radar dish switches off. Notification toggles flip OFF one by one, except a single "Mum" contact. A clock shows one focused hour filling with a warm colour.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); slow, kind final line._

## OUTRO + CTA (3:25–3:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Five videos. One idea. Your focus isn't broken. It's being pulled: by open loops, by feelings, by impossible plans, by switching… and by the little glowing rectangle beside you.
Tell me in the comments where your phone is right now, and I'll see you in the next chapter: the kitchen timer shaped like a tomato that changed how people study and work.

**[VISUAL]** A five-panel recap grid of the series thumbnails, each lighting up as it's named. The final panel is the phone, which dims. End screen: comment prompt "Where's your phone right now? 📱" + next-video card with a tomato-shaped kitchen timer.

_Tone: gentle, reflective on the recap; friendly on the question; a curious lift on the tomato tease._
