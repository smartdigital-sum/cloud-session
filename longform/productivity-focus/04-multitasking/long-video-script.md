## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: Truth 1, Your brain switches (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
Truth one: for anything that needs real thinking, your brain mostly doesn't do two things at once. It switches.
In 2001, psychologists Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans measured this. Every time people switched between two tasks, they lost time. And the more complex the tasks, the more each switch cost.
Each loss is tiny. A fraction of a second. (pause) But do it hundreds of times a day, and those fractions quietly add up.
And sometimes, a fraction of a second is all it takes.

**[VISUAL]** A railway-style track switch inside a stylised brain: a train labelled TASK A stops, the points change with a clunk, a train labelled TASK B starts. A stopwatch ticks a tiny "+0.x s" each time. Speed-up montage: the stopwatch blurs; the small marks pile into a heap. Last line: freeze on a car's brake light.

_Tone: clear, explanatory; ominous on the last line._

### BODY 2: Truth 2, The unicycling clown (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Truth two: when you split your attention, you miss things you'd swear you'd see.
In a 2010 study, researchers at Western Washington University sent a clown on a unicycle across a busy campus square. Bright costume. Big red nose.
Afterwards, they asked people who'd walked through: did you see a unicycling clown?
Of people walking alone, about half said yes. Of people talking on their phones? Only about one in four. (pause) A clown. On a unicycle. Right in front of them.

**[VISUAL]** 2D campus square, students crossing. A clown wobbles through on a unicycle. Split view: a walker without a phone turns their head and smiles; a walker on a call walks straight past, eyes unfocused. Simple icons: 2 in 4 heads tick vs 1 in 4 heads tick. Caption: _Hyman et al., 2010_.

_Tone: playful, building to a deadpan three-beat "A clown. On a unicycle. Right in front of them."_

### REHOOK 1 (1:50)

But walking past a clown is harmless. (pause) The next truth happens behind the wheel.

**[VISUAL]** The campus scene slides away; a dashboard and steering wheel fill the frame.

### BODY 3: Truth 3, Hands-free isn't brain-free (1:50–2:20)

**[NARRATION]**
Truth three: hands-free doesn't mean brain-free.
In a 2006 driving-simulator study at the University of Utah, people drove while talking on a phone, both handheld and hands-free.
They braked later. They reacted slower. And the researchers concluded that talking on a phone while driving can be as impairing as driving drunk, at the American legal limit.
The problem isn't your hands. (pause) It's that your attention is somewhere else.

**[VISUAL]** Driving-simulator screen. A hands-free earpiece glows. The car ahead brakes; a reaction-time bar fills slower for the phone driver. Side panel: a wine glass icon labelled 0.08% (US LIMIT) with an ≈ sign to the phone icon. Last line: the driver's thought bubble floats off to a conversation scene while the road stays empty behind it.

_Tone: serious and steady; no drama, let the facts do it._

**[VISUAL]** Hundreds of tiny figures; a handful start to glow.

### BODY 4: Truth 4, The myth and the supertaskers (2:20–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Truth four is where it gets interesting.
You may have heard that heavy multitasking damages your brain. A famous 2009 Stanford study suggested heavy media multitaskers were worse at filtering out distractions.
But when other scientists tried to repeat it, the results were weak and mixed. So that scary claim? (pause) Not proven.
And then there's the surprise. In 2010, the same Utah team tested two hundred people driving while doing a hard memory task. A tiny group, about two and a half percent, showed no drop at all. They called them "supertaskers".
(pause) Which means ninety-seven and a half percent of us… are not.

**[VISUAL]** A dramatic newspaper headline "MULTITASKING FRIES YOUR BRAIN!" gets a red stamp: **NOT PROVEN**. Then a crowd of 200 small figures in driving seats; almost all of them flash red; 5 glow gold with capes. Caption: **SUPERTASKERS ≈ 2.5%** · _Watson & Strayer, 2010_. Camera finds one red figure: you.

_Tone: intrigued, honest on "not proven"; playful reveal on "supertaskers"; gentle punchline at the end._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So here's the real truth. For thinking tasks, multitasking isn't a superpower most of us have. It's a cost you keep paying.
Walking and talking? Fine. Your body runs on autopilot. But two tasks that both need your mind? Something always loses.
So stop splitting. Batch your messages into set times. Work in single-task blocks. And put your phone out of reach, not just face down.
One thing, fully. (pause) Then the next.

**[VISUAL]** Two lanes: AUTOPILOT + THINKING merge smoothly; THINKING + THINKING collide. Three cards: **BATCH · BLOCK · OUT OF REACH**. The attention spotlight from the hook settles on one object and grows bright and steady. The phone slides into a drawer.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); slow, spaced final line._

## Fact check

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rubinstein, Meyer & Evans (2001): switching between tasks costs time; costs rise with task complexity | ✓ *JEP: Human Perception and Performance* 27(4), 763–797 | [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/oct01/multitask) |
| "A fraction of a second" per switch | ✓ in line with the lab switch costs and Meyer's half-second driving example (APA Monitor) | as above |
| Popular "multitasking costs 40% of productivity" | ✗ **left out**: not found in the 2001 paper or the APA coverage | — |
| Unicycling clown: ~25% of phone talkers vs ~51% of lone walkers noticed (when asked) | ✓ Hyman et al. (2010), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 24(5). ⚠ the costume details are kept generic ("bright costume, big red nose") | [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/talking-mobile-phone-youre-less-likely-notice-unicycling-clown) |
| 2006 simulator: handheld and hands-free phone drivers as impaired as drivers at 0.08% BAC | ✓ Strayer, Drews & Crouch (2006), *Human Factors* 48(2), 40 participants, simulator. 0.08% is the US limit (India's is 0.03%), hence "American legal limit". The authors' own wording is "can be as profound" | [MedicalXpress summary](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2006-06-drivers-cell-bad-drunks.pdf) |
| 2009 Stanford: heavy media multitaskers worse at filtering distractions | ✓ Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009), *PNAS* 106 | — |
| …but replications are weak/mixed | ✓ Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein (2017): only 5 of 14 replication tests significant; meta-analytic association non-significant after small-study correction | [PMC5662702](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5662702) |
| Supertaskers ≈ 2.5% (5 of 200) showed no decline driving + memory/maths task | ✓ Watson & Strayer (2010), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 17 | [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/03/100329075911.htm) |
| Walking + talking fine; two thinking tasks clash | General principle (automatic vs controlled tasks), worded plainly | — |
