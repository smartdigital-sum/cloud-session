## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever picked a restaurant just because it was packed? Or bought something because "ten thousand people bought this"?
That's social proof: when we're unsure, we copy the crowd. Most of the time, it's a smart shortcut.
(pause) But a crowd can be faked. And you'd never see the actors.

**[VISUAL]** A busy food stall next to an empty one; the figure joins the queue. A phone screen: "10,000+ bought this!" Pull back: the crowd at the stall are cardboard cut-outs on wheels. Title card: **SOCIAL PROOF**.

_Tone: knowing; mysterious last line._

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The Asch line experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In the 1950s, psychologist Solomon Asch invited students to a simple "vision test": which of three lines matches the first one? Easy.
But every other person in the room was secretly working with Asch. On certain rounds, they all calmly gave the same wrong answer.
On those rounds, people went along with the wrong answer about a third of the time. And around three in four went along at least once.
(pause) Their eyes were right. The room was louder.

**[VISUAL]** A 1950s classroom: seven students around a table, one real participant at the end. The line card on an easel. Each actor answers "A"; the participant squints, hesitates, then says "A". A tally: ~1/3 OF ROUNDS · ~3 IN 4 AT LEAST ONCE. Caption: _Asch, 1951–1956_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; pointed last line._

### Beat 2: One honest voice (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, most answers were still correct, and about a quarter of people never gave in at all. Later reviews of many studies found that conformity changes with time and culture.
And Asch found something hopeful. When just one other person in the room gave the right answer, conformity dropped sharply.
(pause) One honest voice can break the spell.

**[VISUAL]** A pie chart: most answers CORRECT, a slice WENT ALONG. A quarter of figures stand firm with arms folded. A world map with conformity bars that rise and fall by country and decade. Then one actor in the row says "C"; the participant's shoulders relax and he says "C" too.

_Tone: fair, then hopeful; warm last line._

### Beat 3: Crowds you can buy (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Crowds pull us outside the lab too. In a 1969 New York study, when one person stood on a pavement staring up at a window, a few passers-by stopped. When fifteen people did, around four in ten stopped.
Today, the crowd lives on your screen: star ratings, "bestseller" badges, "2,000 people are viewing this", follower counts. All of it can be faked, with paid reviews, bot followers and invented "live" counters.
In investment scams, it's a chat group full of "members" posting their profits. Many may be the scammer's own accounts.
(pause) A crowd on a screen can be one person with many accounts.

**[VISUAL]** A 1960s street: one man looking up, people walk by; fifteen looking up, a crowd gathers. Caption: _Milgram, Bickman & Berkowitz, 1969_. Cut to a phone: stars, badges, a ticking "2,000 viewing". A chat group: "+₹40,000 today! 🚀" from twenty profiles. Pull back: one person at a desk with twenty phones.

_Tone: knowing, serious; slow last line._

### Beat 4: The crowd isn't always wrong (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Of course, following others is often sensible. A busy food stall is often busy for a reason, and honest reviews can save you money.
In 2022, the Bureau of Indian Standards published a standard to help platforms keep reviews genuine, including disclosing paid ones. But it's voluntary, so you still need your own filter.
(pause) The question isn't "How many people?" It's "Are they real?"

**[VISUAL]** A real, steaming food stall with happy customers. A document stamp: IS 19000:2022 · ONLINE CONSUMER REVIEWS, with a small tag VOLUNTARY. A magnifying glass over a crowd of avatars: some real faces, some blank grey shapes.

_Tone: fair, balanced; firm last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's your defence. When a crowd is pushing you, check whether it's real. Read the worst reviews, not just the best. Look at the dates, and whether reviews sound copied.
Be extra careful with any group where everyone is winning and nobody asks questions.
And remember Asch's room: you can be that one honest voice.
(pause) Don't count the crowd. Check it.

**[VISUAL]** A finger sorts reviews to "lowest first". Five reviews posted the same day, with the same wording, light up in red. A chat group where every message says "profit" gets a ⚠ label. Back in the Asch room, the viewer-figure says "C", and two others nod.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); firm last line._
