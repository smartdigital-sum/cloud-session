**Text**
```
[firmly] A uniform can get you to do things you'd never do on your own. And scammers know it.
[quietly] A man in a grey lab coat says calmly: "Please continue."
[short pause] In 1963, about two out of three people did, all the way to what they believed was a dangerous electric shock.

[knowingly] Have you ever done something just because someone in a uniform, or with an important title, told you to?
That's the pull of authority.
[calm] In this video: the 1963 experiment everyone quotes, what the critics found when they looked closer, and the fake uniforms emptying bank accounts today.
[short pause] [warmly] And a six-word rule that protects you, without making you paranoid.

[intrigued] At Yale University, psychologist Stanley Milgram told volunteers they were helping to study memory. Their job: give a "learner" an electric shock for every wrong answer, rising step by step to four hundred and fifty volts.
The learner was an actor, and the shocks weren't real. But the volunteers didn't know that. As the learner protested, the experimenter calmly said things like, "The experiment requires that you continue."
In the version Milgram first reported, twenty-six of forty people, about sixty-five percent, went all the way to the end.
[short pause] No threats. No force. Just a man in a lab coat.

[thoughtful] But the story isn't that simple. Milgram ran many versions of the experiment, and obedience varied a lot. When orders came by phone, or when other "volunteers" refused, far fewer people went to the end.
Psychologist Gina Perry studied the archives and argued that many participants doubted the shocks were real, and that experimenters sometimes pushed beyond the script.
Still, in 2009, Jerry Burger ran a safer, partial repeat that stopped at one hundred and fifty volts. About seventy percent were willing to go past that point.
[short pause] The exact numbers are debated. The pull is real.

[serious] That was a lab. [short pause] Today, the lab coat has been swapped for a police uniform on a video call.

[serious] Scammers know this. A video call from someone in a police uniform, with a badge, an office backdrop, even a fake "court order". A caller who says he's from your bank's "head office", or a government department.
They borrow the look of authority because they don't have the real thing. And they mix it with fear and urgency, so you don't stop to check.
Indian authorities have warned: no real officer will "arrest" you over a video call, or ask you to transfer money to clear your name.
[short pause] A uniform on a screen is just a costume.

[gently] So should you distrust every uniform? [short pause] That would be its own mistake.

[warmly] To be fair, trusting real experts is usually sensible. We should listen to doctors, pilots and firefighters.
[thoughtful] And even in Milgram's lab, people didn't obey blindly. Many argued, hesitated, and some refused outright. And when others refused first, saying no became much easier.
[short pause] The question isn't "Is this person in charge?" It's "Can I check?"

[warmly] So here's your defence. When someone uses a title, a uniform or an official logo to rush you, verify through a second channel. Hang up, find the official number yourself, and call back.
Real authority can wait while you check. Fake authority gets angry when you do.
And if you've been targeted, report it on one-nine-three-zero, or at cybercrime dot gov dot in.
[long pause] Respect the role. Verify the person.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: have you, or someone you know, ever had a call from a fake "official"?
[short pause] And next: why fake reviews and crowds fool us so easily.
```
