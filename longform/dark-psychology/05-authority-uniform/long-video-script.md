## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The 1963 shock experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
At Yale University, psychologist Stanley Milgram told volunteers they were helping to study memory. Their job: give a "learner" an electric shock for every wrong answer, rising step by step to 450 volts.
The learner was an actor, and the shocks weren't real. But the volunteers didn't know that. As the learner protested, the experimenter calmly said things like, "The experiment requires that you continue."
In the version Milgram first reported, 26 of 40 people, about 65 percent, went all the way to the end.
(pause) No threats. No force. Just a man in a lab coat.

**[VISUAL]** A 1960s lab: the shock generator with its row of switches, a wall between the volunteer and the learner. The experimenter stands calm and still. A counter fills up: 26 of 40 figures reach the last switch. Caption: _Milgram, 1963_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller, darkening; slow last line._

### BODY 2: What the critics found (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
But the story isn't that simple. Milgram ran many versions of the experiment, and obedience varied a lot. When orders came by phone, or when other "volunteers" refused, far fewer people went to the end.
Psychologist Gina Perry studied the archives and argued that many participants doubted the shocks were real, and that experimenters sometimes pushed beyond the script.
Still, in 2009, Jerry Burger ran a safer, partial repeat that stopped at 150 volts. About 70 percent were willing to go past that point.
(pause) The exact numbers are debated. The pull is real.

**[VISUAL]** A wall of index cards, one per version of the experiment, each with a different bar height. A phone replaces the lab coat; the bar drops. Archive boxes open; question marks rise from old questionnaires. Then a modern lab with a "STOP AT 150V" sign. Captions: _Perry, 2012_ · _Burger, 2009_.

_Tone: fair, balanced; firm last line._

### BODY 3: Fake uniforms, real money (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Scammers know this. A video call from someone in a police uniform, with a badge, an office backdrop, even a fake "court order". A caller who says he's from your bank's "head office", or a government department.
They borrow the look of authority because they don't have the real thing. And they mix it with fear and urgency, so you don't stop to check.
Indian authorities have warned: no real officer will "arrest" you over a video call, or ask you to transfer money to clear your name.
(pause) A uniform on a screen is just a costume.

**[VISUAL]** A video call frame: a "police officer", a flag, an official seal. Pull back to reveal a cheap studio: a costume rack, a printed backdrop, a seal stuck on with tape. A fake "court order" PDF with a smudged stamp. An advisory card: NO "DIGITAL ARREST" BY VIDEO CALL.

_Tone: serious, protective; slow last line._

### REHOOK 2 (2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
So should you distrust every uniform? (pause) That would be its own mistake.

**[VISUAL]** The phone screen fades; a real doctor and a pilot step into frame.

### BODY 4: Authority isn't the enemy (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, trusting real experts is usually sensible. We should listen to doctors, pilots and firefighters.
And even in Milgram's lab, people didn't obey blindly. Many argued, hesitated, and some refused outright. And when others refused first, saying no became much easier.
(pause) The question isn't "Is this person in charge?" It's "Can I check?"

**[VISUAL]** A doctor explaining a scan, a pilot at the cockpit door: warm, trustworthy. Back in the 1960s lab, a volunteer pushes the chair back and stands up. A second figure stands, then a third. A big checkmark labelled CAN I VERIFY?

_Tone: fair, warm; thoughtful last line._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

So here's your defence. When someone uses a title, a uniform or an official logo to rush you, verify through a second channel. Hang up, find the official number yourself, and call back.
Real authority can wait while you check. Fake authority gets angry when you do.
And if you've been targeted, report it on 1930, or at cybercrime.gov.in.
(pause) Respect the role. Verify the person.
