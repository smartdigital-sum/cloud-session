**Text**
```
[firmly] A free gift can buy a much bigger yes from you than it ever cost.
[curious] A stranger hands you a free cold drink. A few minutes later, he asks you to buy raffle tickets for his cause.
[short pause] In one famous experiment, people in your place bought about twice as many. The drink was the reason.

[knowingly] Have you ever found it hard to walk out of a shop after they've offered you free tea?
That's reciprocity: the feeling that we must repay what we're given.
[calm] In this video: a 1971 experiment with a drink worth a few cents, why the pull is so strong, and the "free" offers that are really bait.
[short pause] [warmly] And the one sentence that lets you keep the gift, and still say no.

[intrigued] In 1971, psychologist Dennis Regan ran an experiment at Cornell University. Participants thought they were simply rating paintings, alongside another student who was secretly working with the researcher.
During a short break, that "student" left the room. For some participants, he came back with two bottles of Coke: "I got one for you too." For others, he came back with nothing.
Later, he asked each participant to buy raffle tickets, at twenty-five cents each. Those who'd been given a Coke bought, on average, about twice as many.
[short pause] A drink worth about ten cents had bought a much bigger "yes".

[thoughtful] Sociologist Alvin Gouldner argued that some version of this rule, "repay what you receive", is found across human societies. It's how people learn to trust and cooperate.
But it has a weak spot. The pull to repay can switch on even when we never asked for the gift, and even when we didn't want it.
And the debt can feel bigger than the gift. In Regan's study, many people paid back far more than that Coke was worth.
[short pause] The gift is small. The pressure isn't.

[leaning in] And it's not just cold drinks. [short pause] The same pull is built into offers you see every day.

[knowingly] You'll see it everywhere. In a 2002 restaurant study, diners who got a sweet with the bill left bigger tips. When the server gave one sweet, walked away, then turned back to offer a second, "just for you", tips rose the most.
Free samples in supermarkets. A "free" health check-up that ends in a sales pitch. A stranger at the ATM who "helps" you, then asks for a favour.
[serious] In scams, it's a "free gift" parcel that needs only a small delivery fee, or a prize that needs a "processing charge".
[short pause] The freebie is the hook. The ask is the catch.

[gently] But does that mean every gift is a trap? [short pause] No. And the difference matters.

[warmly] To be fair, most gifts are just kindness. Reciprocity is what makes friendship, family and community work.
[thoughtful] It becomes manipulation when the gift is a tool: given only to create a debt, and followed quickly by a request you'd normally refuse.
Watch the timing. A gift, then an ask, close together, especially from someone you've only just met.
[short pause] Real kindness doesn't send you a bill.

[warmly] So here's your defence. Accept real gifts warmly. But if a gift turns out to be a sales tool, call it that, in your own head.
Once you see it as a tactic and not a favour, the feeling that you owe something starts to fade. You can say "thank you" and still say "no".
And never pay a fee to receive something "free".
[long pause] A gift with a hook isn't a gift. It's bait.

[friendly] Tell me in the comments: what's the sneakiest "free" offer you've ever been given?
[short pause] And next: why we obey a uniform, and the famous experiment that still divides psychologists.
```
