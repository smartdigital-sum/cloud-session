## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: The 1971 Coke experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1971, psychologist Dennis Regan ran an experiment at Cornell University. Participants thought they were simply rating paintings, alongside another student who was secretly working with the researcher.
During a short break, that "student" left the room. For some participants, he came back with two bottles of Coke: "I got one for you too." For others, he came back with nothing.
Later, he asked each participant to buy raffle tickets, at twenty-five cents each. Those who'd been given a Coke bought, on average, about twice as many.
(pause) A drink worth about ten cents had bought a much bigger "yes".

**[VISUAL]** A 1970s lab room with paintings on easels. Two chairs: the real participant and a "student" with a hidden badge reading WORKS WITH RESEARCHER. Split screen: in one room he returns with two Cokes, in the other with empty hands. Ticket counters: 1 vs 2. Caption: _Regan, 1971_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; pointed last line._

### BODY 2: Why the pull is so strong (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Sociologist Alvin Gouldner argued that some version of this rule, "repay what you receive", is found across human societies. It's how people learn to trust and cooperate.
But it has a weak spot. The pull to repay can switch on even when we never asked for the gift, and even when we didn't want it.
And the debt can feel bigger than the gift. In Regan's study, many people paid back far more than that Coke was worth.
(pause) The gift is small. The pressure isn't.

**[VISUAL]** A map of the world with tiny gift-and-return arrows lighting up across villages and cities. Then an unwanted parcel lands on a doorstep; a glowing thread runs from it to the homeowner's wrist. A scale: a tiny bottle on one side, a heavy coin bag on the other, and the scale still tips toward "OWE".

_Tone: thoughtful; quiet emphasis on the last line._

### BODY 3: Sweets, samples and "helpful" strangers (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
You'll see it everywhere. In a 2002 restaurant study, diners who got a sweet with the bill left bigger tips. When the server gave one sweet, walked away, then turned back to offer a second, "just for you", tips rose the most.
Free samples in supermarkets. A "free" health check-up that ends in a sales pitch. A stranger at the ATM who "helps" you, then asks for a favour.
In scams, it's a "free gift" parcel that needs only a small delivery fee, or a prize that needs a "processing charge".
(pause) The freebie is the hook. The ask is the catch.

**[VISUAL]** A restaurant bill tray with one sweet, then two; a server turning back with a wink. Quick cuts: a sample tray, a check-up camp banner reading FREE that flips to PACKAGE ₹9,999, a helpful hand at an ATM. A phone message: "Congratulations! Pay ₹99 to receive your FREE gift." A fishing hook glints inside the gift box.

_Tone: knowing, serious; slow last line._

**[NARRATION]**
But does that mean every gift is a trap? (pause) No. And the difference matters.

### BODY 4: Not every gift is a trap (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, most gifts are just kindness. Reciprocity is what makes friendship, family and community work.
It becomes manipulation when the gift is a tool: given only to create a debt, and followed quickly by a request you'd normally refuse.
Watch the timing. A gift, then an ask, close together, especially from someone you've only just met.
(pause) Real kindness doesn't send you a bill.

**[VISUAL]** Warm scenes: neighbours sharing food over a wall, friends splitting a bill. Then a timeline: GIFT → (a few minutes) → ASK, with a red bracket labelled TOO CLOSE. A gift box with a price tag tucked under the ribbon.

_Tone: fair, warm; firm last line._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
So here's your defence. Accept real gifts warmly. But if a gift turns out to be a sales tool, call it that, in your own head.
Once you see it as a tactic and not a favour, the feeling that you owe something starts to fade. You can say "thank you" and still say "no".
And never pay a fee to receive something "free".
(pause) A gift with a hook isn't a gift. It's bait.

**[VISUAL]** The viewer-figure turns the gift box over and reads a hidden label: TACTIC. The glowing thread on their wrist snaps. They smile, say "Thank you", and hand back the raffle tickets. A "PAY ₹99 FOR FREE GIFT" message gets a big ✗.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); firm last line._
