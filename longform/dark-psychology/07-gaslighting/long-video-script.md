Have you ever walked away from an argument unsure what had actually happened, even though you were there?

(pause) But the word is used so often now that its real meaning gets lost. So what is it, really?

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: Where the word came from (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
The word comes from a 1938 play, "Gas Light", and its famous 1944 film version, "Gaslight".
A husband secretly dims the gas lamps in the house and hides things. When his wife notices, he insists she's imagining it, and that she's becoming forgetful and unwell.
His goal is to make her doubt her own mind, so she depends on him and stops asking questions.
(pause) The cruelty wasn't just the lie. It was teaching her to distrust herself.

**[VISUAL]** A theatre poster: GAS LIGHT · 1938. A black-and-white film reel: GASLIGHT · 1944. A hand turns a gas valve in the attic; downstairs, the lamp dims. A brooch vanishes from a table. The wife's reflection in a mirror becomes faint and unsure.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller, darkening; quiet last line._

### Beat 2: What it looks like (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Gaslighting is a pattern, not one argument. Common signs: being told "That never happened" about things you clearly remember. "You're too sensitive" whenever you raise a concern. Your memory, your feelings, or your sanity questioned again and again.
Over time, you might start apologising constantly, second-guessing small decisions, or checking with that person before trusting yourself.
(pause) The goal isn't to win one argument. It's to become the judge of what's real.

**[VISUAL]** Speech bubbles stack up: "THAT NEVER HAPPENED." · "YOU'RE TOO SENSITIVE." · "YOU'RE REMEMBERING IT WRONG." The figure shrinks a little with each one, a "SORRY" bubble appearing more and more often. A judge's gavel passes from the figure's hand to someone else's.

_Tone: thoughtful, serious; firm last line._

### Beat 3: What research says, and doesn't (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
To be honest, research on gaslighting is still young. It isn't a medical diagnosis, and there's no simple test for it.
Some researchers, like sociologist Paige Sweet, argue it works best where there's already a power imbalance: in relationships, families or workplaces.
And not every disagreement is gaslighting. People remember things differently, and everyone is wrong sometimes.
(pause) One disagreement is normal. A pattern that wears down your trust in yourself is the warning sign.

**[VISUAL]** A thin stack of research papers labelled STILL GROWING. A see-saw tilted heavily to one side, labelled POWER. Two friends cheerfully disagreeing about what colour a car was. Then a single disagreement (a small dot) versus a long chain of dots wrapping around a figure.

_Tone: honest, balanced; clear last line._

### Beat 4: Don't diagnose, document (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So this video won't help you label anyone. That's not the point. The point is to protect your own sense of reality.
One practical step: keep a private record. Write down what happened, when, and what was said, soon after it happens. Save messages. Keep it somewhere safe that only you can reach. If it's happening at work, keep copies of emails and notes from meetings too.
(pause) A record doesn't argue. It just remembers.

**[VISUAL]** A "DIAGNOSE" stamp is set aside. A notebook opens: a date, a time, a short note. Screenshots slide into a locked folder. The gas lamp from the hook steadies and brightens a little.

_Tone: warm, practical; gentle last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

And talk to someone you trust outside the situation: a friend, a family member, a counsellor. Gaslighting works best in isolation.

In India, the domestic violence law also recognises emotional abuse.
(pause) Your memory is allowed to be yours.
