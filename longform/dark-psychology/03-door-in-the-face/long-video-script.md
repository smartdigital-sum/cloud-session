## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The 1975 zoo-trip experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1975, psychologist Robert Cialdini and his team stopped students on a university campus.
First, they asked them to counsel young offenders for two hours a week, for at least two years. Almost everyone refused.
Then came the real request: would you take a group of these young people on a one-day trip to the zoo? About half agreed. When students were asked only about the zoo trip, only about one in six said yes.
(pause) The impossible request wasn't a mistake. It was the setup.

**[VISUAL]** A 1970s campus path. A researcher with a clipboard; a "2 YEARS × 2 HRS/WEEK" card gets a big ✗. Then a small zoo-ticket card: half the crowd raise hands. A second crowd, asked only about the zoo: 1 in 6 hands. Caption: _Cialdini et al., 1975_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; pointed last line._

### Beat 2: Why a big "no" leads to a "yes" (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Cialdini's explanation: reciprocal concessions. When someone "gives up" their big request, it feels like they've made a concession, a step back for you.
And we feel pressure to step back too, to meet them halfway. There's also contrast: after something huge, the smaller request suddenly looks reasonable.
(pause) Your "yes" feels like your choice. But the stage was built for it.

**[VISUAL]** Two figures on a bridge; one takes a step back, labelled CONCESSION; the viewer-figure feels a tug and steps forward to meet them. A giant boulder next to a pebble makes the pebble look tiny (CONTRAST). Then a theatre stage with the "YES" spotlight already set up before the figure walks on.

_Tone: thoughtful; quietly revealing last line._

### Beat 3: Bargains, plans and relatives (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
You'll see it in bargaining: a shopkeeper's opening price that's clearly absurd, so his "final offer" feels generous.
In "premium" plans shown first, so the middle plan looks sensible. In a relative who asks for a huge loan, then "only" a smaller one.
And unlike some famous findings, this one has been repeated. A preregistered replication published decades later found a similar effect.
(pause) It's not a myth. It's a tool.

**[VISUAL]** A bazaar stall: "₹2,000!" → "OK, ₹800, final." A pricing page: PREMIUM ₹999 · STANDARD ₹499 (highlighted) · BASIC ₹199. A phone chat: "Can you lend ₹2 lakh?" → "OK, just ₹20,000?" A REPLICATED ✓ stamp on an old journal page.

_Tone: knowing, confident._

### Beat 4: When it becomes manipulation (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Of course, not every big-then-small request is a trick. Sometimes people genuinely ask high, then adjust. Negotiation is normal.
It becomes manipulation when the first request was never meant to be accepted. When it's built only to make you feel you owe a concession.
Watch for the pattern: a huge ask, an instant "compromise", and pressure to agree right now, before you've had a moment to think.
(pause) The test isn't the size of the ask. It's whether the first one was real.

**[VISUAL]** Two scenes: a fair negotiation with both sides writing real numbers, and a rigged one where the first "offer" card is a cardboard prop. A magnifying glass labelled WAS IT REAL? hovers over the first request.

_Tone: fair, thoughtful; firm last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's your defence. When a smaller request follows a big refusal, wipe the first one from your mind.
Judge the second request completely on its own. Ask: "If this had been the only thing they asked, would I say yes?"
And remember: refusing something unreasonable doesn't create a debt.
(pause) You don't owe anyone a "yes" for saying "no".

**[VISUAL]** An eraser wipes the first request off a whiteboard, leaving only the second. A ledger titled I OWE stays empty. The figure walks away calmly, door closed behind them by their own hand.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); firm, kind last line._
