## MAIN BODY

### BODY 1: Three steps, one flip (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
Psychologist Jennifer Freyd described DARVO in 1997. It stands for Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender.
Deny: "That never happened." Attack: "You're always looking for problems. What's wrong with you?" Reverse: "I'm the one being hurt here. Do you know how this makes *me* feel?"
In under a minute, the person who raised the harm is the one on trial.
(pause) The conversation didn't end. It was turned around.

**[VISUAL]** Three cards flip over in turn: **DENY** (a hand waving it away), **ATTACK** (a finger pointing at the speaker), **REVERSE VICTIM & OFFENDER** (two name tags, VICTIM and OFFENDER, swapped between the two figures). Caption: _Freyd, 1997_.

_Tone: intrigued, clear; quotes delivered flat, not acted; pointed last line._

### BODY 2: Why it sways onlookers too (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
DARVO doesn't only confuse the person who spoke up. It can sway everyone watching: friends, family, colleagues.
Studies from Freyd's lab found that when people saw DARVO in a scenario, they rated the real victim as less believable, and the person using DARVO as more believable.
Another study linked experiencing DARVO with more self-blame.
(pause) That's the quiet power of the flip: you start doubting yourself.

**[VISUAL]** An audience of silhouettes watching the two figures. Believability meters above each figure: the victim's drops, the other's rises. Then the victim figure alone at night, a thought bubble: "MAYBE IT WAS MY FAULT?" Caption: _Harsey, Zurbriggen & Freyd, 2017_.

_Tone: thoughtful, serious; quiet last line._

### BODY 3: Where you'll see it (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
You'll see versions of it in families, workplaces and relationships. Raise a concern about someone's behaviour, and suddenly you're the one "creating drama". In a group, it can turn the whole room against the person who spoke up.
You'll see it in public too: someone accused of wrongdoing who instantly attacks the accuser and claims to be the real victim.
And it often overlaps with gaslighting, from video seven: first denying what happened, then questioning your memory.
(pause) The pattern is the same. Only the stage changes.

**[VISUAL]** Three small stages side by side: a family dinner, an office meeting room ("You're creating drama"), a press podium with flashing cameras (no real people, generic figures). A dim gas lamp from video 07 flickers in the corner. A spotlight sweeps across all three stages, showing the same three cards on each.

_Tone: knowing, serious; firm last line._

### BODY 4: Not every defence is DARVO (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, people who are wrongly accused also deny it, and they may feel genuinely hurt. Denial alone isn't DARVO.
And research on DARVO is still growing. Many of the studies use college students and imagined scenarios.
The warning sign is the full pattern: deny, attack your character, then take the victim's place, especially when it happens again and again.
(pause) So don't rush to label people. Watch the pattern.

**[VISUAL]** A figure says "That's not true" calmly, with open hands, and no attack follows: a green tick. A thin stack of papers labelled RESEARCH: GROWING, with a small "college samples" note. Then a looping arrow repeating DENY → ATTACK → REVERSE three times: a red ⚠.

_Tone: fair, balanced; clear last line._

## CLOSING (3:00–3:30)

_Pays off the hook and the lock-in promise._

**[NARRATION]**
And here's the hopeful part. In a 2020 study, Sarah Harsey and Jennifer Freyd found that people who had learned about DARVO were less swayed by it. They saw the real victim as more believable.
Simply knowing the name helps. So when you notice the flip, name it to yourself: "We were talking about what happened to me." Bring the conversation back, write it down, and talk to someone you trust. If you ever feel unsafe, the helplines are on screen.
(pause) Once you see the flip, it loses its grip.

**[VISUAL]** The audience from Beat 2 is handed a small card reading DARVO; their believability meters swing back. The victim figure turns the arrow back round to the original topic. A notebook, a friend's hand on a shoulder. Small on screen: **If you feel unsafe: 112 · 181 · Tele-MANAS 14416**.

_Tone: warm, hopeful, empowering (peak); firm last line._
