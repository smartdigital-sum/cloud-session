## HOOK (0:00–0:15)

**[NARRATION]**
Would you let someone put a huge, ugly "Drive Carefully" sign on your front lawn?
(pause) Most people said no. Unless, two weeks earlier, they'd said yes to something tiny.

**[VISUAL]** A neat suburban house with a perfect lawn. A giant, crooked, hand-painted "DRIVE CAREFULLY" board swings into frame and blocks most of the house. Rewind effect: a tiny square sign in the window, two weeks earlier.

_Tone: curious, playful; intriguing turn on "Unless…"._

## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Have you ever agreed to "just one small favour", and somehow ended up doing far more than you planned?
That's the foot-in-the-door technique. Salespeople, scammers, and sometimes people close to us use it.
(pause) Once you see it, you can step back before the door swings open.

**[VISUAL]** A door opens a crack; a shoe slides into the gap. The door slowly swings wider as more requests stack up behind it. Title card: **FOOT-IN-THE-DOOR**.

_Tone: knowing, protective._

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The 1966 lawn-sign experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In 1966, psychologists Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser ran a famous field experiment in California.
A researcher asked homeowners to let them put a big, badly lettered "Drive Carefully" sign on their lawn.
Asked straight away, only about one in six agreed. But homeowners who, two weeks earlier, had agreed to put a small sign in their window? Most of them said yes.
(pause) A small yes opened the door to a big one.

**[VISUAL]** Two streets side by side. Street A: a researcher knocks once; only 1 of 6 houses gets the big sign. Street B: two weeks earlier, small window signs go up; later, most houses accept the big sign. Caption: _Freedman & Fraser, 1966_.

_Tone: intrigued storyteller; satisfying last line._

### Beat 2: Why a small yes changes you (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
Why? One explanation is self-image. After the first yes, people start to see themselves as "the kind of person who supports safe driving", or "the kind of person who helps".
Saying no to the bigger request would clash with that new picture of themselves. We like to stay consistent with what we've already done.
(pause) The first request isn't really about the small thing. It's about changing how you see yourself.

**[VISUAL]** A homeowner looks in a mirror after placing the small sign; the reflection gains a badge "SAFE-DRIVING SUPPORTER". When the big request comes, the reflection frowns at the idea of saying no. A chain of footprints labelled CONSISTENT leads forward.

_Tone: thoughtful; quiet emphasis on the last line._

### Beat 3: Where you'll see it (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
You'll see it everywhere. A "free" sign-up that only needs your phone number, then your address, then your card.
An online "friend" who first asks for a small recharge, then a loan. A "quick survey" at your door that ends in a sales pitch.
In scams, it's often the first step: a tiny payment of one rupee to "verify" your account.
(pause) Each step feels small. Together, they walk you somewhere you never chose to go.

**[VISUAL]** A staircase: each step labelled with a request (PHONE NUMBER → ADDRESS → CARD; ₹1 VERIFY → ₹500 → ₹50,000). A figure climbs step by step without looking up; at the top, a dark doorway. Pull back to show how high they've climbed.

_Tone: knowing, serious; slow last line._

### Beat 4: How strong is it, really? (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
To be fair, the effect isn't magic. Later reviews of many studies found it's real, but usually smaller than in that famous lawn study.
It works best when the first and second requests feel connected, and when the first one is done freely.
(pause) And it's not always sinister. Friends ask small favours all the time. The question is where the steps are leading.

**[VISUAL]** A large "76%!" headline shrinks to a modest bar labelled TYPICAL EFFECT. Two puzzle pieces (REQUEST 1 · REQUEST 2) click together when connected and fail to fit when unrelated. A friendly neighbour borrowing sugar, contrasted with a staircase into the dark.

_Tone: fair, balanced._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So here's your defence. When a request comes after an earlier "yes", pause and judge it on its own.
Ask: "If this were the very first thing they'd asked, would I agree?" If not, you can say no, even if you said yes before.
Your past yes doesn't own your next decision. And you can stop halfway up the stairs. Changing your mind is allowed.
(pause) Consistency is a strength, until someone else is steering it.

**[VISUAL]** The figure stops mid-staircase and looks at the next step in isolation (a spotlight on one request). A clean "NO" card appears. The shoe in the doorway from the setup is gently pushed back out; the door closes.

_Tone: warm, empowering (peak); firm last line._
