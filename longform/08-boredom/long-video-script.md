## HOOK (0:00–0:15)

**[NARRATION]**
In a famous experiment, people sat alone in a room, with nothing to do but think, for about fifteen minutes.
There was one button. It gave them an electric shock.
(pause) Many of them pressed it.

**[VISUAL]** A bare, white room. One chair, one table, one red button wired to an ankle band. A wall clock ticks loudly. A hand hovers… presses. A small cartoon zap and a startled face. Title flash: **15 MINUTES. NOTHING TO DO.**

_Tone: hushed and intriguing; deadpan on "Many of them pressed it."_

## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
Last time, we saw that experts protect their rest. But today, most of us never let our minds rest at all.
A queue. A lift. Two minutes waiting for the kettle. The phone comes out, every single time.
We've deleted every empty moment from our day.
(pause) What if those empty moments were doing something important?

**[VISUAL]** Quick cuts: a person in a queue, in a lift, by a boiling kettle, at a bus stop; each time, the phone rises into frame and the background goes grey. A day timeline fills up completely, no gaps left. A small blank square tries to appear and gets instantly filled by a phone icon.

_Tone: calm, gently knowing; curious lift on the last line._

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: The 15-minute experiment (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
That experiment was run in 2014, by psychologist Timothy Wilson and his team at the University of Virginia.
Earlier, many participants had said they'd even pay money to avoid that shock. Yet left alone with their thoughts, about two in three men and one in four women shocked themselves at least once.
Some scientists push back. The shock was mild, they say, and some people were probably just curious.
(pause) But the point stands: sitting with nothing to do feels surprisingly hard.

**[VISUAL]** The white room again, now with a caption: _Wilson et al., 2014_. A "WOULD PAY TO AVOID" sign over a cartoon shock. Two simple icon rows: 2 of 3 men highlighted; 1 of 4 women highlighted. Then a second scientist character raises a hand with a speech bubble: "MILD SHOCK? CURIOSITY?". The room's clock slows; the person fidgets.

_Tone: clear, slightly amused; fair on the pushback; thoughtful on the last line._

### Beat 2: Bored people get more ideas (1:15–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
So boredom feels bad. But it might be doing something useful.
In 2014, psychologists Sandi Mann and Rebekah Cadman in England gave some people a truly boring job: copying numbers out of a phone book.
Then everyone had to think of uses for a pair of plastic cups.
The bored group came up with more ideas. And when people just read the numbers, without even writing, they did better still.
(pause) The more room the mind had to drift, the more ideas it found.

**[VISUAL]** An old, thick phone book; a hand copies numbers line by line, eyes glazing. Cut to two plastic cups on a table; idea bubbles pop up (phone on a string, plant pot, drum). A simple bar: CONTROL < COPYING < READING. Caption: _Mann & Cadman, 2014_.

_Tone: curious, lightly playful on the phone book; satisfying on the last line._

### Beat 3: Why ideas arrive in the shower (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Ever had your best idea in the shower? There's a reason.
In 2012, researchers at the University of California, Santa Barbara gave people a creative problem, then a twelve-minute break.
Some did a hard task. Some rested. Some did an easy, boring task that let their minds wander.
When they came back to the same problem, the easy-task group improved the most.
(pause) A wandering mind isn't switched off. It's quietly working in the background.

**[VISUAL]** A head in profile under a shower; small gears turn slowly inside it, then a lightbulb blinks on. Cut to three break rooms: HARD TASK (tense), REST (lying down), EASY TASK (sorting coloured shapes, eyes drifting to clouds). Bars on return: EASY TASK highest. Caption: _Baird et al., 2012_.

_Tone: warm and curious; slow, almost secretive on the last line._

### Beat 4: The honest part (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Now, honesty. These are small studies, and creativity is hard to measure. And boredom isn't always good. Being bored all the time, for months, is linked to real problems.
So the lesson isn't "be bored more".
It's this: the short, empty moments we used to have, waiting, walking, staring out of a window, may have been where our minds sorted, rested and connected ideas.
(pause) And we've handed every one of them to the phone.

**[VISUAL]** A "SMALL STUDIES" sticker and a wobbly measuring tape around a lightbulb. A grey, heavy cloud labelled CHRONIC BOREDOM sits apart from a small, light cloud labelled SHORT GAPS. Montage of old gaps: a bus window, a quiet walk, a person gazing out at rain; each gap gets swallowed by a phone screen sliding in. Callback: the faint glowing thread from video 05.

_Tone: honest and balanced; quiet, slightly sad on the last line._

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So bring back the gaps. Not hours. Minutes.
Stand in the queue without your phone. Walk without a podcast, once in a while. Between your pomodoros, look out of a window instead of at a screen.
Do the dishes. Fold clothes. Let your mind drift.
And when an idea arrives, write it down. (pause) Boredom isn't empty time. It's your mind making room.

**[VISUAL]** The day timeline from the setup reopens: small white gaps appear between blocks. In each gap: a queue without a phone, a walk with no earphones, a window glance between tomato timers (06), dishes, folded clothes. A tiny notebook catches a glowing idea. Final frame: a calm face looking out of a window as small ideas float in.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); slow and gentle on the last line._
