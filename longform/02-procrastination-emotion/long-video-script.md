## HOOK (0:00–0:15)

**[NARRATION]**
You'll clean your whole room. Reply to old messages. Reorganise your phone.
(pause) Anything… except the one thing you're supposed to do.
That's not laziness. Lazy people don't work *this* hard to avoid work.

**[VISUAL]** Fast 2D montage: a figure scrubbing a spotless room, rearranging app icons, folding clothes at speed. In the corner, one untouched laptop with a blinking cursor. Freeze-frame on the figure looking at the laptop, then away.

_Tone: dry, knowing smile; quick rhythm on the list, slow on "except the one thing"._

## SETUP (0:15–0:40)

**[NARRATION]**
In the last video, we saw how unfinished tasks keep pulling at your mind. So why do we keep putting them off?
For years, we were told it's a time-management problem. Get a better planner. Wake up earlier.
But the researchers who study procrastination say it's something else.
(pause) It's a *mood*-management problem.

**[VISUAL]** The orbiting sticky notes from video 01 flash past. Planners, alarm clocks and productivity apps pile up, then get crossed out with a red line. Big title card: **NOT A TIME PROBLEM → A MOOD PROBLEM**.

_Tone: steady; let "mood" land with weight._

## MAIN BODY

### Beat 1: What really happens (0:40–1:15)

**[NARRATION]**
Here's what really happens.
You open the task. And something small hits you. Boredom. Confusion. A quiet voice saying, "What if I do this badly?"
So you switch tabs. And instantly… you feel better.
That relief is the trap. Your brain just learned: avoid the task, lose the bad feeling.
Two of the leading researchers in this field describe procrastination as putting short-term mood repair ahead of long-term goals.
In simple words: you're not running from the work. (pause) You're running from a *feeling*.

**[VISUAL]** Close-up: the laptop opens; small grey storm clouds (labelled BORED, CONFUSED, "WHAT IF I FAIL?") drift out. Click: a new tab with videos; the clouds vanish, a little sun appears. A simple loop diagram draws itself: **TASK → BAD FEELING → AVOID → RELIEF** ↺. Caption: _Sirois & Pychyl, 2013_.

_Tone: curious, then gentle insight on the last line._

### Beat 2: Future you (1:15–1:45)

**[NARRATION]**
And who pays for that relief? Future you.
In a 2009 brain-scan study at Stanford, when people thought about their future self, their brain activity looked less like thinking about "me"… and a little more like thinking about someone else.
So when you say, "I'll do it tomorrow," part of you is handing the job to a stranger.
(pause) A stranger who happens to be you.

**[VISUAL]** The figure hands a heavy box labelled THE TASK across a calendar line to a hazy, faceless silhouette marked TOMORROW. Inset: a stylised brain scan (no real data), "ME" glowing bright, "FUTURE ME" dimmer. The silhouette turns: it's the same figure, looking tired.

_Tone: thoughtful; a slight sting on "who happens to be you."_

### Beat 3: The cost (1:45–2:20)

**[NARRATION]**
That loan comes with interest.
In 1997, psychologists Dianne Tice and Roy Baumeister followed college students through a semester. Early on, the procrastinators were doing great. Less stress. Fewer illnesses.
But by the end of the term, everything flipped. They were more stressed, sicker, and got lower grades.
The relief was real. It was just *borrowed*… from the version of them that had to pay it back.

**[VISUAL]** Two students on a semester timeline. Week 1: the procrastinator relaxing in a hammock, the other one studying. The timeline scrolls; stress meters cross over. End of term: the procrastinator with a thermometer, a stack of books and a red grade. A receipt prints: **RELIEF, PAID WITH INTEREST**.

_Tone: serious, measured; slow on "borrowed."_

### Beat 4: The guilt trap (2:20–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So what do most of us do next? We get angry at ourselves. "Why am I so lazy? What's wrong with me?"
But think about it. Guilt is just another bad feeling. And what do you do with bad feelings? (pause) You avoid them. With more procrastination.
In 2010, researchers at Carleton University followed students across two exams. Those who had procrastinated badly before the first exam, but then *forgave* themselves, procrastinated less before the second.
It's one small study. But it points somewhere surprising: shame may not fix procrastination. (pause) It may *feed* it.

**[VISUAL]** The loop diagram returns; a new cloud labelled GUILT joins and the loop spins faster. Cut to two exam dates on a calendar; a student writes "I forgive myself" on a sticky note and the second exam's storm cloud shrinks. Caption: _Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett, 2010_.

_Tone: empathetic, almost a gentle laugh on "What's wrong with me?"; careful and honest on "one small study."_

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So the cure isn't more discipline. It's two small, kind moves.
One: forgive yourself for yesterday. Out loud, if you need to.
Two: shrink the task until the feeling shrinks with it. Not "write the report." Just "open the file." Not "study chapter five." Just "read one page."
You don't need to feel ready to start. (pause) Very often, the feeling comes *after* you start.

**[VISUAL]** Two cards flip: **1 FORGIVE** · **2 SHRINK**. A huge task block ("WRITE THE REPORT") shrinks step by step to a tiny pebble ("OPEN THE FILE"); the storm cloud shrinks with it. The figure clicks the file open; a small sun rises.

_Tone: emotional peak, warm and certain; long pause before the last line._

## OUTRO (3:30–4:00)

**[NARRATION]**
So next time you're avoiding something, don't ask, "Why am I so lazy?"
Ask, "What am I feeling?" (pause) Then make the first step small enough to beat it.
Tell me in the comments: what's the task you're avoiding right now… and what's the feeling behind it?
And subscribe. Next: why your Monday plans fall apart by Wednesday.

**[VISUAL]** Back to the clean room from the hook; the figure sits at the laptop, calm. Text swaps: ~~WHY AM I LAZY?~~ → **WHAT AM I FEELING?** End screen: comment prompt + next-video card **Why Your Monday Plans Collapse by Wednesday**.

_Tone: gentle, practical; friendly on the comment question._
