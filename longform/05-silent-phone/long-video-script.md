## MAIN BODY

### Pull 1: The "brain drain" study (0:40–1:20)

**[NARRATION]**
In 2017, researchers at the University of Texas at Austin ran a simple test. Hundreds of people did tasks that measure working memory and problem solving. Every phone was on silent.
The only difference was where the phone was. On the desk, face down. In a pocket or bag. Or in another room.
The people whose phone was in another room did best. The ones with it on the desk did worst.
And here's the strange part: most of them said they weren't thinking about their phone at all.
The researchers called it "brain drain". (pause) But this is where honesty matters.

**[VISUAL]** Three identical desks side by side. Desk A: phone face down. Desk B: phone peeking out of a bag. Desk C: phone shown in a separate room through a doorway. A simple "brainpower" bar above each person: C fullest, A lowest (illustrative, no exact values). A thought bubble over Desk A's person: "Phone? What phone?" Caption: _Ward et al., 2017_.

_Tone: curious and clear; a light surprise on "the strange part"; then slow down for "honesty matters."_

### What the replications found (1:20–1:50)

**[NARRATION]**
When another team repeated the experiment in 2022, they found… no difference.
A 2023 review of more than twenty studies did find an overall cost from having a phone nearby. But the size of it varied a lot.
So the fair answer is: the effect is probably real, but likely smaller and less certain than the headlines said.
(pause) The next two pulls, though, you can see in your own life.

**[VISUAL]** The bars from Pull 1 flicker. A second lab with three desks: all bars equal, stamp **NO DIFFERENCE (2022)**. Then a stack of 20+ paper icons, scale tipping slightly toward "COST", with a wobbly needle. Final card: **PROBABLY REAL · PROBABLY SMALLER**.

_Tone: honest, measured; no drama._

### Pull 2: The buzz you don't answer (1:50–2:25)

**[NARRATION]**
Pull two: the buzz you don't answer.
In 2015, researchers at Florida State University gave people a task that needs steady attention. During it, some of them got a call or text notification on their own phone. They didn't pick it up.
They still made more mistakes. And the drop was similar in size to what earlier studies found when people actually used their phones.
Why? Because one buzz opens a loop: "Who was that?" (pause) And as we saw in the first video, open loops don't stay quiet.

**[VISUAL]** A person doing a fast-paced task (numbers flashing on screen, tapping a key). The phone on the desk lights up with a buzz; the person doesn't touch it, but a small "WHO WAS THAT?" bubble appears and the next few taps turn red. Callback: the glowing open-loop ring from video 01 forms around the bubble. Caption: _Stothart et al., 2015_.

_Tone: curious; gentle callback on the last line._

### Pull 3: Phantom vibrations (2:25–3:00)

**[NARRATION]**
Pull three: your brain starts listening for it.
Have you ever felt your phone vibrate… and it hadn't?
In one study, nearly nine in ten university students said it had happened to them. In a hospital survey, about two in three doctors and staff.
It's mostly harmless. But it shows something: part of your attention is always on standby. Waiting for the next buzz.

**[VISUAL]** A figure pats their pocket, pulls out the phone: blank screen, no notifications. A puzzled "?" pops up. Two simple icon rows: 9 of 10 students highlighted; 2 of 3 hospital staff highlighted. A small radar dish on the figure's head slowly sweeps, labelled STANDBY.

_Tone: playful on the question, then thoughtful on "standby."_

## PAYOFF / TWIST (3:00–3:30)

**[NARRATION]**
So what actually works? Not more willpower. Distance.
For your most important hour of the day, put your phone in another room. Not face down. Not in your pocket. Gone.
Turn off every notification that isn't from a real person. Check messages at set times, the way we batched them last video.
You don't need to resist something that isn't there. (pause) Distance does the work for you.

**[VISUAL]** The figure stands, walks the phone to another room, closes the door. Back at the desk, the glowing thread from the hook snaps and fades; the radar dish switches off. Notification toggles flip OFF one by one, except a single "Mum" contact. A clock shows one focused hour filling with a warm colour.

_Tone: warm certainty (peak); slow, kind final line._
